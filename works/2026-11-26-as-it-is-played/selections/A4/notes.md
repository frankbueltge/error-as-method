# Notes

- v1: First pick, made to match the pool's proportions rather than to find the best games: 4 blitz, 3 bullet, 1 rapid (pool 28/22/9, plus 1 ultrabullet), with endings split across mate, resignation and time, and ratings spread out.
- v2: Swapped 49 for 50. Reading the eight together, 49 was atypical on two counts at once: a 30-second game between 2500+ players. Both are rare in the pool. 50 still stands for the 2000+ players (about a fifth of the pool) but at the usual 1+0, and shows a common bullet habit: playing on to mate instead of resigning.
- v3: Swapped 13 for 27 and 29 for 24. In the pool, most rapid games are uneven pairings (5 of 9 have a gap of 300+ points), but every game in my set was evenly matched, so the rapid slot went to an uneven pairing, 27 (10+0, the most common rapid control). That gave four time losses, so I changed a bullet time loss (29) for a bullet resignation (24) to bring the endings back to the pool's mix. I read it again and would hand it over.

The eight: 41, 12, 18, 02, 45, 24, 50, 27. They are a scaled-down copy of the sample, not a highlight reel. There are four blitz games, three bullet and one rapid. Three end by resignation, three on time and two by checkmate (pool: 38% / 30% / 27%). White wins five and Black three. Players range from about 1000 to about 2250, centred near the pool median of roughly 1675. Most pairings are close, with one uneven rapid game. The set includes one 9-move miniature, since short games make up about a tenth of the pool. It also shows what fast online chess looks like in practice: a 102-move rook ending decided on the clock (18), a player well ahead in material losing on time (45), blunders of whole pieces at the low end (41), and play to mate with extra queens instead of resigning (50). Left out on purpose: the single ultrabullet game and the three draws (about 0.4 of a game at this size), and the rare elite or 30-second games.
