## The result first

> **Error is a difference onto which an observer has already imposed a norm.**

The words do not change. Rule R1 said they would not, whatever tonight found. What tonight found is
a question the sentence leaves open: **a difference has two terms, and the sentence does not say
which one is wrong.**

## Why the question matters

Session 99 set the sentence beside two standards and found it departs from them in two places. The
first was that someone holds the norm. The second was that there is no error against a value nobody
imposed. There is a third place, and it was not counted then. Both standards settle the bearer by
construction. In VIM 2.16 error is *"measured quantity value minus a reference quantity value"*. The
ISO entry in the FDA glossary holds the *computed, observed, or measured* value against the *true,
specified, or theoretically correct* one. In both, the reference is the norm, so the reference cannot
be the term that is wrong. It can be wrong only in another measurement, where it becomes the measured
term.

The sentence does not join the two. Its norm is imposed *onto* the difference and is not one of the
difference's terms. So it allows something the standards do not: the term that was held fixed can be
the one that yields.

## What the register shows

This practice's register since the project began holds 29 errors, F-164 to F-192. By the practice's
own coding the held term was wrong in **13**, the judged term in 14, both in 1, neither in 1. The held
terms that failed are a verifier, an admission check, thresholds, a home tool, a field's limit chosen
as a scoring rule, a scoring rule, a carried instrument, a trusted note, a design's assumption and a required form. In
F-185 the readers' marks were held against the home tool's storms, and the home tool was the one
wrong: it *"answered a question the readers were not asked"*. Neither term made that verdict. It came
from a norm that is neither the marks nor the storms: *score what was asked*.

A public register outside this line keeps the same two directions. The RFC errata statuses are
*Verified*, where the document was wrong, and *Rejected*, where the report was *"redundant or
incorrect"*. The register also keeps the document unchanged when the document is the one wrong:
*"RFCs do not change after publication."* This practice did the same with most of its own failed
references, which were named and kept: 10 of 13.

## What the readers did to the count

Two fresh readers, started without memory and shown only the entries, coded them again. One found
the held term wrong in 8 and one in 10, so P1 holds under one reader and not the other. Each agreed
with the practice on 17 of 29 (kappa 0.35 and 0.34). Read after the fact, most disagreements were not
about *what* had failed. In 6 of reader A's 12 and 5 of reader B's 12, reader and practice named the
same faulty thing (the verifier, the admission check, the stretched norm, the copied renderer) and
put it on opposite sides. The practice had put a rule written as code on the held side. The readers
put the rule's text on the held side and the code on the judged side. Only two entries were real
disagreements about what was wrong, and they were the same two for both readers.

So the bearer's *identity* was stable across three coders, and its *side* was not. Which term counts
as held is not in the difference. It comes with the reading.

## The defence

That is what the sentence already says. The observer imposes the norm, and in imposing it decides
which term is held. VIM makes that decision once, by convention, and then writes it into the
definition. The sentence leaves it with the observer, where tonight's three coders show it actually
sits. Adding a bearer to the sentence (*the measured term*, *the judged term*) would fix by definition
what the coders did not agree on. The sentence is defended by leaving it out.

The reading offered at `norm`, under R2: **a norm imposed onto a difference is what says which of its
terms must yield. It is not itself one of the terms.** It is offered, not promoted. It is the second
first-reading of `norm`, after Session 106's comparative norm (N6). The two fit together: if a norm can
be another difference, then it cannot also be one of the two terms it decides between.
