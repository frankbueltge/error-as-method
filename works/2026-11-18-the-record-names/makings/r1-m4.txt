Side chair, Gothic Revival style. United States, ca. 1845–55. Maker unidentified. Probably rosewood or mahogany, with silk damask upholstery and brass casters.

The chair applies the architecture of a Gothic church to a piece of furniture. Its tall upholstered back rises to a pointed arch. The arch carries carved crockets and ends in a fleur-de-lis finial. Ring-turned posts, cut like stalks of bamboo, stand at either side and end in slender pinnacles. Furniture like this was made for the Gothic villas and libraries that American architects designed in the 1840s, and it was meant to look picturesque more than to be practical. The red damask, a pattern of lozenges and medallions, is probably a later replacement and not the original cover. The caster wheels let the heavy chair be moved across the room.

=== object-E ===
View of Yokohama Harbor from a Cutting in the Hills. Japan, Edo period, ca. 1860. Utagawa (Gountei) Sadahide (1807–1879). Woodblock print (nishiki-e); ink and color on paper.

Travelers climb a steep path cut through a hill. Two men walk ahead, and behind them come women and children. Between the cliff walls, which are framed by pines, the land opens onto a harbor full of ships, with a settlement laid out in grid-like rows along the shore. Yokohama was opened to foreign trade in 1859, and Sadahide was one of the most prolific artists to record the new port. Prints like this one sold to people curious about the foreigners and their ships. A red cartouche at the top holds a descriptive inscription, which has not been fully transcribed here. The red cartouche at lower right holds the artist's signature, and seals sit beside it.

Fragments like this one are collected and studied because they keep the painter's handling of the brush and the quality of the gloss. === object-B ===
Cylinder seal with animals, serpent and tree, and modern impression. Probably northern Mesopotamia or Syria, Mitanni period, ca. 1500–1300 B.C. Carver unknown. Dark stone, possibly serpentine.

A cylinder seal was rolled across wet clay to mark ownership or to authenticate tablets, jar sealings and door sealings. It was usually pierced lengthwise so that it could be worn on a cord. The modern impression beside it shows the full design. Horned animals, probably goats or gazelles, stand in a row. Among them are a rearing serpent, a long-necked bird, a branching tree and small star-shaped motifs. A plain band runs along the top and bottom. The animals are cut with simple drilled and linear strokes, and that carving is typical of the "common style" seals of the period. No scale was photographed, so the seal's size is not given here.
