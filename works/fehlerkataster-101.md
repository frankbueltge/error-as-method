## F-321 — the people's commonest word taken for what a first association is

**What happened.** H2 predicted that the six instances' most frequent word would be the people's most frequent word
on at least 16 of 24 cues. It was on 8. `PREDICTIONS.md` named this as the outcome that would surprise the practice
most, and set the alarm at 12. H4 rested on the same assumption: with the people's first word struck, the family
would move to the people's second word on at least 8 cues. It did on 7. R1's margin (at least 30 of 48 unanimous;
28 came) was an optimistic bet on the same picture: one family tracing one human peak.

**Whose error.** The practice's, in design. It held the norm *the people's commonest word is the right first
associate* before anything was measured, and then used the norm to predict the machine. Read without that norm, the
difference is a finding. The family has a peak of its own (`S153.OWNPEAK`). In the standing sentence's terms, this
is the *already*: the observer imposed the norm, and the difference measured did not have it. The predictions stand
as committed (`214feec`).

## F-322 — a scoring rule that cuts words

**What happened.** `usf.norm` drops a final *s* from any word of four letters or more not ending in *ss*, so that
*books* and *book* count as one. It also cuts *indigenous* to *indigenou*, *physics* to *physic*, *rebellious* to
*rebelliou* and *clothes* to *clothe*. The rule is applied the same way to the makers' words and the people's, so no
agreement count changes. One case is untested: a people's word and a maker's word that differ only by the cut would
match. That can only raise the overlap with people, and no reported number depends on such a pair (checked by hand
for the 24 S cues).

**Whose error.** The practice's, in the rule fixed before the run. It is not changed tonight, because the
predictions were scored by it. The face shows the makers' words as written and says that the people's words are
shown as scored.

## F-323 — a count of people taken as a count of participants

**What happened.** The norms list only responses given by two or more participants. The probability that three
people agree, and the six-person distinct count, treat every remaining participant as having written a word no one
else wrote. That is how the norms define idiosyncratic responses (Intro, *"given by a single participant"*). But
`p_three_agree` sums over targets after the scoring rule merged some of them (`CREATED` and `CREATE` stay apart;
`CLOTHES` and `CLOTH` would merge). Merging can only raise the people's agreement. So the 1.68 is, if anything,
generous to the people, and the gap to 16 is not an artefact of it.

**Whose error.** The practice's, as a limit of the reference it built. It is recorded here because the line has
spent three nights saying it lacked a human reference. Now that it has one, the reference's own construction has to
be on the record too.
