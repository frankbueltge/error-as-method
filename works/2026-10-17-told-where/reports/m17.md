I made "Sixty-One Exposures", a still image in the style of a cyanotype (sun print), and wrote it to /tmp/claude-0/s133/mk/m17/work/index.html. NOTE.md is in the same folder. The page uses plain canvas and JavaScript, with the extracted data embedded in it. It loads nothing from outside: no URLs in the file, and no outside requests when I rendered it in headless Chromium at 1100 × 800.

**What I did first:** I looked at the image, then printed its pixels as a text map of darkness. Two things stood out. Along the bottom there is a band of zero values, broken by a regular gap about every 4.8 px; each gap is one day's daylight, and there are about 61 of them. And the dots have no clear upper limit to their values in the image, so I can only read heights relative to the highest dot.

**From the file to the form:**
1. I found each dot as a cluster of dark pixels and took its centre. A cluster about twice as dark as a single dot was counted as two dots that had merged. I measured each height relative to the zero line and the highest dot.
2. I worked out the day length in pixels from where the daytime gaps fall (4.82 px per day). My first guess was a little off, and the daylight appeared to slide sideways by about 4–5 hours over the record, so I fixed it.
3. I lined the days up on the middle of their daylight ("solar noon"). I dropped a few faint specks at night, which look like JPEG noise.
4. On the page, each day is one horizontal line, top to bottom: dawn at left, noon in the centre, dusk at right. Each hour's light adds blue, so the nights stay bare paper. A ragged dark-blue border stands for the brushed coating that sat in full sun.
5. The top and bottom lines are partial days, because the record starts in an afternoon and ends before the next afternoon.

**What the commissioner should know:**
- The values are reconstructed from a 300 × 90 JPEG, at about 4.8 values per pixel column. That puts hour positions about ±1 h out, heights a few percent out, and the order of hours within one column is uncertain.
- Very small dawn and dusk values merge into the zero line. Only about 760 of the expected non-zero dots could be told apart.
- So the work is faithful to how each day looks and how the days compare, not to individual hourly values.
- The only text on the image is a small pencil-style title and a one-line key under the print.

**My view of what was measured:** it fits sunlight at a fairly high latitude, with the season moving towards winter. It rests on these readings:
- **Daylight is short:** about 12 lit hours per day in the first two-thirds, and about 10 in the last third.
- **It is getting shorter:** the lit span narrows over the 61 days.
- **The strongest days are weakening:** the highest clear-day peaks fall by about 8–10% (top five peaks about 0.93 at the start, about 0.85 at the end).
- **Total daily light** is lower in the last third.
- **Clouds show clearly:** clear days are tall and smooth, and cloudy days are low and scattered.

My guess is a mid-latitude site in roughly late summer into autumn (or the southern hemisphere's equivalent), but this is weak. The scale is unknown, and very weak dawn and dusk light may be hidden, so day lengths could be understated. Noon falls a few hours after each day's first sample in the series' own clock. That could mean timestamps in another time zone such as UTC, but I can't tell where the record starts closely enough to say so.
