I made "Sixty-One Days of Light". It is one standalone canvas page with no external resources, and it rendered at 1100 × 800 in headless Chromium with no errors and no network requests.

**What I did first.** I looked at the image, then dumped its pixels as text so I could read them row by row. Two things stood out:
- A thick dark floor line at rows 83–84 has a short gap every ~4.92 px. 300 px ÷ 61 days = 4.92 px, so the gaps repeat exactly once a day.
- Above the floor, dots scatter up to a ceiling that changes from day to day.

**From the file to the form.** There are about 4.9 hourly values per pixel column, and each dot is about 2 px wide. That means the hour-by-hour values cannot be recovered from the picture, so I did not invent them.
- I fitted the timing of the daily gaps (daylight centres at x ≈ 1.57 + 4.918·d).
- I cut the strip into 61 windows of 24 hours, each centred on a daytime. The first and last are half-days, because the record starts and ends partway through a day.
- For each day I measured how much ink sits at each height, and the highest dark pixel. For the 60 windows centred on nights, I measured how much ink sits on the floor line.

The work shows 61 strands of warm light rising from a horizon, one per day. Each strand is bright wherever that day's dots fell, and a bead marks the day's highest point. Below the horizon, 60 thin blue lines hang between the strands, one per night, with length set by the floor ink. All values are drawn from the measured ink and are embedded in the page as a small JSON block. The page has a small caption naming Fort Peck, 634 m and 1,464 hours.

**Things the commissioner should know:**
- The strands show how the record's ink is spread by height. They are not reconstructed hourly values; the 300 × 90 JPEG cannot give those back.
- The night lengths are the least reliable part. Near-floor values at dawn and dusk also count as floor ink, so the small rise in nights around days 44–49 may partly reflect dull days rather than longer nights.
- The first and last strands are half-days on purpose.

**What I think was measured: incoming solar radiation (global irradiance), probably over roughly mid-June to mid-August.** This rests on what the file shows:
1. **Daily rhythm:** the series goes back to the same flat floor (zero) once every 24 hours and leaves it once a day. A floor level that many hours of every day sit on fits nighttime sunlight. Temperature or wind would not do this.
2. **Long days:** the floor ink fits roughly 7 hours a day at the floor, with about 17 hours above it. That suits high summer at about 48° N.
3. **Season turning:** the daily ceilings are high and fairly even over the first half, then sink steadily over the last 20 or so days (top pixel from about row 13 to about row 25–32). That fits the period after the solstice.
4. **Cloudy days:** some days are clearly lower and broken up (days 1, 3, 8 and 49, for example).
5. **A short spike:** around days 24–25 a short burst sits above every other day. This is typical of brief cloud-edge brightening in irradiance data.

The gaps are not centred where local noon would fall in the record's own hourly grid. My guess is that the timestamps are in UTC (solar noon at Fort Peck is around 19:00 UTC), but I cannot check this without knowing the start hour.

Separately, I know from memory, not from any lookup, that a solar radiation monitoring station exists at Fort Peck, Montana at 634 m. That fits, but my reading rests on the file evidence above.
