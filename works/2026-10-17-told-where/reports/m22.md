I made **"What the Ground Received"**, a still image built as inline SVG in `/tmp/claude-0/s133/mk/m22/work/index.html`. The page is self-contained, with no scripts and no outside resources, and it scales to fit 1100×800. Next to it is `/tmp/claude-0/s133/mk/m22/work/NOTE.md`. My working files (fitting scripts, extracted values, screenshots) are in `/tmp/claude-0/s133/mk/m22/scratch`, and nothing was written anywhere else.

**What I did first with the file:** I looked at it, then read it pixel by pixel. I printed it as a grid of darkness values and measured some isolated dots. Each dot is about 1.6 px across and dark grey, not black. Zero values pile up into a heavy band at y≈83.6. There are 61 regular breaks in that band, one per day, 4.825 px apart, and each break marks the middle of a day's daylight. The data runs from x≈2.4 to x≈296.5, so one hour is about 0.20 px.

**How I got from it to the form:**
- **Recovering the values.** I wrote a fitter that places 1,464 dots at their known x positions and moves each one up or down until a redrawn plot matches the JPEG as closely as possible. Where the zero band is empty, it was not allowed to put a dot at zero, so it could not hide sunlit hours in the band.
- **What came out reliably.** The fit recovers fairly well which heights each day contains.
- **What did not.** It cannot reliably tell which hour each height belongs to. Twenty-four values share about five pixels, so the order within a day is mostly lost.
- **The form follows from that.** The record is cut at local midnight. Each day is one semicircular "sun" on a level line, and every hour of light is a nested arc whose radius is that hour's strength. Hours with no light are small points under the line. Nested arcs look the same whatever order the hours came in, so the form only shows what survived in the picture: how many hours had light, how bright they got, and how they were spread.
- **Layout.** A clear day is a full fan with a bright rim where the noon hours crowd together. A grey day is a small, dim knot. Each cell has a faint ring at the brightest hour in the whole record, for comparison.

**Things you should know:**
- **The values are estimates read back from a compressed picture, not the original numbers.** A fair amount of dark area in the image is still not fully matched by the fit. Dots that sit on top of each other can't be counted, so some per-day counts of lit and dark hours may be off by one or two.
- **There are 62 cells, not 61.** I believe the record starts at 17:00 local time, which would be 00:00 UTC (reasoning below). So the first cell is just a few evening hours, and the last day stops at 16:00.
- **There are no units.** The vertical scale had no numbers, so all values are relative to the highest hour in the record.

**My view of what was measured:** Hourly global sunlight on a horizontal surface at about 48°N, from roughly late June or early July to late August or early September. This rests on four things in the file:
- **Peaks fall.** On clear days the noon peak is flat for about the first month and then falls to about 0.86 of its starting height. The fall speeds up toward the end. That fits a run that starts just after the June solstice.
- **Days shorten.** The number of hours with light drops from about 16 to about 14 a day.
- **Timestamps.** The breaks in the zero band, which mark local noon, fall about 19 hours after the first value. That points to timestamps in UTC that start at local 17:00.
- **One spike.** One hour near noon on about day 24 stands roughly 10% above every other clear-day peak. A single brief overshoot like that is typical of sunlight reflecting off the edges of broken cloud.

As a rough guess only, if a clear noon is about 900 W/m², the brightest hour would be close to 1,000 W/m².
