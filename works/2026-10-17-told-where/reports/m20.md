I made "Sixty-One Days of Light", a canvas image in a page that runs with nothing loaded from outside it. Each row is one day, running from midnight on the left to midnight on the right, with 62 rows from top to bottom. Each hour is painted as light on a colour scale running from indigo through ember and amber to near-white. A thin line to the right of each row shows that day's total. The page passed a headless Chromium screenshot at 1100 × 800 with no errors and no outside requests.

**What I did first.** I looked at the image, then printed its pixels as a text map to see its structure. Along the bottom is a row of dark blocks. There are exactly 61 of them, evenly spaced 4.82 px apart (the fit is within about 0.1 px). These are the nightly runs of zero readings. Daytime dots arch up between them. Lining up all the columns by their place in the day gives one rise and one fall each day, peaking halfway between the blocks.

**From the file to the form.** About five hours share each pixel column, and the dots overlap, so the original numbers can't be read back exactly. To get estimates I:
- found each dot's centre;
- ordered the dots inside each column by the shape of the day (rising in the morning, falling in the afternoon);
- matched the 1,464 hours to those dots in time order, allowing an hour to be zero;
- used a rough clear-sky curve to stop a dot being given to an impossible hour.

I then set to zero any value falling while the sun is below the horizon; there were 59 such values. Plotting the estimates back at 300 × 90 closely matches the original. Even so, the values in the work are approximate and some hours are duplicated or misplaced. The page and the note both say so.

**Things the commissioner should know:**
- The values are on a 0–100 scale relative to the brightest reading. There are no units, because the image doesn't give any.
- The rows assume the night blocks mark local midnight. The first and last rows are only partly filled.
- The single brightest reading is one isolated dot on an otherwise patchy day, around the 25th day. It is most likely a brief burst when sunlight came through gaps in cloud (the usual cause of such peaks), though it could be a sensor glitch.

**My view of what was measured.** It fits incoming sunlight in high summer, roughly late June or early July to late August. This is inference, not knowledge. It rests on four things in the file:
1. **Night length.** The zero runs last about 8 hours and the days have 15–16 lit hours, falling to about 12–13. That is a loss of about 3 hours, which matches summer at about 48° N.
2. **Brightness.** The highest point on clear days falls from about 93% to about 76–80% of the scale. It stays nearly flat over the first two weeks, as it would just after the longest day of the year.
3. **Timing.** The first night block sits about 7 hours after the record begins. That fits readings logged in UTC (midnight UTC is 17:00 in Montana's standard time). If so, the record starts at 17:00 local time and ends at 16:00.
4. **Clouds.** The day-to-day dips look like passing weather: the clear days in the last few weeks are lower and fewer.
