I turned the record into a still image called **Sixty-One Suns**: 61 days of sunlight shown as rows of half-dials, one per day. There was no SVG renderer here, so I checked the layout only with close approximations drawn in Python, and I haven't seen the page in a browser.

**What I did first.** I looked at the picture, then printed its pixel darkness as text grids and as numbers. Two things stood out right away:
- Along the bottom (the zero line) there are exactly **61 dark blocks**. These are the nights' zero values, about 4.82 pixels apart, which is about 0.201 pixels per hour.
- The daytime dots above them get lower toward the right.

**From the file to the form.**
1. I measured each night block's edges to a fraction of a pixel and took its centre as solar midnight.
2. I found 649 daytime dots by looking for local dark peaks and taking their weighted centres. Height in the picture became a value from 0 to 1, where 1 is the highest dot in the record. The left-right position became an hour of the day, measured from the surrounding midnights.
3. I cut the strip at each midnight. Each day becomes a half-dial: midnight at the bottom, noon at the top, morning on the left. Each hourly value is a 15° wedge, the angle the sun moves in an hour, as long as that hour's light. The wedges overlap with a lightening blend.
4. I tried two other versions first, and both were worse. Joining the dots into a closed outline made spurious spikes, because hours that fall in the same pixel column can't be put in order. Drawing each hour as a thin line looked too sparse.

**What the commissioner should know.**
- **Some hours are missing.** In the original about five hours share each pixel column, so dots with similar values merge. I recovered about 649 daytime dots, against perhaps 700 or more real daytime hours.
- **Hour positions are approximate.** Each placement is good to roughly ±1 hour. Choosing wedges that are each an hour wide is partly an honest way of showing that blur.
- **No units.** Values are relative to the brightest hour in the record. The picture carries no units, so the faint arc on each dial means "the record's brightest hour", not a number of watts.
- **The ends are partial days.** The record starts around 5 pm solar time and ends around 3 pm, 61 days later. So the first dial holds only a few evening hours and the last has no evening. The grid has 62 cells, with two empty at the end.
- **The page has a little text:** a title, a one-line key at the top and a one-line note at the bottom.

**What I think was measured.** It looks like hourly sunlight at a single place, running from late summer into autumn, away from the tropics. This rests on three things in the file:
- **The noons get lower.** On the clearest days, the noon peak falls from about 0.9 to 0.94 of the maximum in the first three weeks to about 0.7 to 0.8 in the last ten days.
- **The nights get longer.** The night blocks widen from about 2.9–3.1 pixels to 3.6–3.9 pixels. Measured from the first and last non-zero hours, daylight shrinks from about 13 to 13.5 hours to about 10.5 to 11, though the per-day figure is noisy. A change of a few hours in two months points to a mid-latitude place, near an equinox.
- **The weather varies day to day.** Clear days give smooth, evenly stepped values, and other days are low and ragged, which looks like cloud.

It could equally be the southern-hemisphere version, roughly February to April. The way the record starts and ends partway through the afternoon suggests its timestamps are not local time, perhaps UTC. I won't guess the place: the ±1 hour error is too large to read a longitude from it.
