**What I did first with the file**
I looked at the image, then turned every pixel into ASCII text so I could read it row by row. Two things stood out:
- **A hard floor.** At the bottom (rows 83–84) there's a dense band made of short dark blocks that repeat. A fitted wave puts their spacing at 4.82 px. The data fills about x = 2 to 297, which gives about 0.2 px per hour, so that spacing is 24 values. The record falls to the exact same lowest value once every day, 61 times.
- **Daily repeats above the floor.** Above the band the dots are scattered, but in some stretches near-identical heights come back every 24 values.

**How I got to the form**
- **What can be recovered.** An hour is only about 0.2 px wide and a dot is about 2 px, so the 1,464 separate values can't be read back. What can be read is how each day's ink is spread up and down.
- **Cutting the days.** I split the image into 61 windows of 24 hours each, lined up to the fitted spacing. For each window I measured the ink at each height, the highest point it reached, and the ink on the floor.
- **The form.** Each day becomes one glowing slit standing on a horizon line. Brightness at each height follows how much ink sat there. A thin bright cap marks the day's highest point. Below the line hangs a dark blue column whose length is in direct proportion to that day's floor ink.

**What the commissioner should know**
- **It's built from the picture, not the numbers.** Every height and length comes from how dark the JPEG is, including blur and compression smudge. The original values can't be recovered from this file.
- **The floor columns are only relative.** They show which days had more hours at the floor, but I couldn't turn them into hour counts. The overlap and size of the dots aren't known well enough.
- **The words on the page are mine.** I added a small title and a one-line caption. The source image has no text at all.

**My view of what was measured**
I think it's sunlight: probably solar irradiance at the ground, or the output of something powered by it, such as solar panels. It covers late summer into autumn, at a site away from the equator. This rests on four things in the file:
1. **A flat zero every day.** The record drops to exactly the same floor every 24 values, and it's an absolute floor, not just a daily low. That fits night.
2. **Daily repeats with irregular day-to-day changes.** Values return to the same heights at the same hour on clear stretches. The size of each day jumps around without any weekly pattern, which looks like cloud rather than a human schedule.
3. **Two slow trends across the 61 days.**
   - The daily top falls: it sits around image rows 9–15 in the first weeks and around rows 21–32 at the end.
   - The ink on the floor grows by about 20%, from roughly 5.0 to 6.0.

   So the peaks get lower and the nights get longer, which is what happens to daylight in the months after midsummer.
4. **One extreme spike.** On day 25 a single value rises well above anything else in the record. That's consistent with the brief overshoot that can happen at the edge of a passing cloud.

There's also a weaker hint that the timestamps aren't local solar time. Each day's floor centres about 4–5 hours after the 24-value boundary that the record's first point sets, which may point to a clock like UTC. I'd give that one little weight.
