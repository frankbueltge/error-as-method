**What I did first:** I looked at the image, then printed every pixel as text to see what it is made of. A few things were clear from that:
- Each dot is about 2 × 2 pixels.
- Each pixel column holds about 4.9 hours, so the dots for neighbouring hours overlap a lot.
- The night-time zeros merge into one band on rows 83–84. That band breaks every 4.92 pixels, which is exactly one day (300 pixels / 61 days).

Because of the overlap, the 1,464 values can't be read back one by one. Any table of "hourly values" I produced would be partly made up.

**How I got to the form:** Since I couldn't recover the numbers honestly, the work keeps the plot itself. It embeds all 300 × 90 pixels and bends them into a circle:
- Time runs clockwise from a thin line at the top, where the record starts and ends.
- Height above the zero line becomes distance from a small central ring. Every night falls onto that faint ring, and every day rises off it as a burst of light, 61 in all.
- Colour runs from ember near the ground to pale white at the highest hours, with a soft glow.

My first try drew each dark pixel as a dot. It looked dim and grainy, so I switched to sampling the record directly into the circle. I chose a circle because the material is made of days that drop to zero and come back, and a ring makes that rhythm the shape itself.

**What you should know:**
- The image also shows the JPEG's blockiness and its 2-pixel dots, because nothing was smoothed into invented values.
- The days are narrow, about 6° each, so they read as a crown of light rather than 61 clearly separate petals.
- There is a small caption in the lower left (title plus two lines on how to read it). It can be removed if you want the image with no words at all.

**My view of what was measured:** This is consistent with hourly sunlight reaching a level surface, somewhere at mid latitudes, from late summer into autumn of whichever hemisphere. That season reading is the least certain part. What it rests on:
- **Night and day:** values drop to zero for long stretches once per 4.92-pixel period. Each day the zero run covers about 4 of the 4.92 pixels, which suggests roughly 11–13 hours of zero and so around 11–13 hours of daylight, but the dot width makes this rough.
- **Clear versus cloudy days:** some days rise high and evenly, others are low and broken. Day 0 is the faintest, and roughly days 1, 3, 8, 9, 18, 25, 30 and 49 are also clearly dimmer.
- **One spike:** around day 24 a single cluster reaches the very top of the plot, well above the other days. It could be a brief burst of extra brightness at the edge of a cloud, or a glitch.
- **Season:** the run of clear-day peaks (80th percentile over sliding 15-day windows) falls steadily from about 72 to about 61 units on a scale of 83, roughly 15% over the two months. The zero band also gets slightly heavier in the later two-thirds. Together these suggest the sun getting lower and nights getting longer.
- **Timing:** the middle of daylight falls about 7.3 hours after the start of each 24-hour cycle. If the record starts at midnight UTC, the site would be around 70°E. That inference is weak, because the start time isn't known.
