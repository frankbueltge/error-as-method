The work is finished: a still image called "Aperture" in `/tmp/claude-0/s133/mk/m16/work/index.html`, with `/tmp/claude-0/s133/mk/m16/work/NOTE.md` beside it. It folds the 61-day record into a dial with one ring per day, the first outermost, and noon at the top.

**What I did first.** I looked at the image and printed it out as text, pixel by pixel. The clearest structure was along the bottom edge: a row of short dark runs at the lowest value, repeating about every 4.8 pixels. That is 300 pixels ÷ 61 days, so the record drops to the same floor once a day, and values above that floor bunch together in the gaps between the runs.

**How I got to the form.**
- I measured where each day's group of raised values is centred. They sit very regularly, 4.824 px apart, starting at x ≈ 6.2.
- I found 623 individual dots above the floor and worked out each one's position to a fraction of a pixel, which places it to within about an hour or two. From its position I got its day and its hour relative to that day's centre (taken as noon), and from its height its value on a 0–1 scale. The floor is 0 and the highest dot is 1.
- These dots are the only data in the page, written inline. Each one is drawn as an arc one hour long on its day's ring, brighter and warmer for larger values. Night values are left dark, and there is a faint dial structure and a short caption. I checked it in headless Chromium at 1100×800: no errors, and it makes no network or file requests.

**What you should know.**
- The page does not hold the original 1,464 numbers. It holds what can be recovered from a small JPEG.
- One pixel covers about 5 hours and the dots overlap, so exact hourly order and exact values are lost. Bunched dots merge, so there are fewer dots per day than real hours.
- Edge dots blur outward, so each day's lit opening looks somewhat wider than it really was.
- The value scale is relative. It assumes the lowest row is zero, and the units are unknown.
- The noon direction is taken from the data itself, not from a clock. Where those centres fall suggests the record's hours may run on UTC (starting near 17:00 local time), but that is only a guess.

**What I think was measured: sunlight reaching the ground (incoming solar radiation), from late autumn up to about the winter solstice, roughly late October to late December.** This rests on:
- **A flat floor every night**, exactly once per 24 values, with no variation. Something that is exactly zero at night and positive by day points to sunlight.
- **Some days with low peaks** next to days at the full top level, which looks like cloud cover.
- **Short days.** Comparing the ink above the floor with the ink of single dots gives about 9–10 non-zero hours per day. That fits winter at 48°N.
- **The season trend.** The span of non-zero hours narrows from about ±6 h around noon at the start to about ±5 h at the end. Daily peaks fall from about 0.9 to about 0.7, and the rough daily total falls from about 5–6 to about 3.5. Both point to days getting shorter, so autumn rather than spring.

One thing doesn't fit neatly: the peaks fall less than clear-sky sunlight at that latitude normally would over two months. So the exact dates, and whether the floor is truly zero, are less certain than the overall picture.
