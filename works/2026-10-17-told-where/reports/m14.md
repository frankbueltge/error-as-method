I made a spiral called **The Night Widens**. The 1,464 hours are wound one day to a turn, so there are 61 rings: the first hour is at the centre and the last is at the rim. Each hour is one cell, coloured from dark ember (low) to warm white (high). The hours that sit on the record's floor are left almost black, and together they make a dark wedge at the bottom that clearly opens wider from the centre out to the rim. The page has no external resources: it is canvas and plain JavaScript with the 1,464 values written into it. I loaded it in headless Chromium at 1100 × 800 and the only file it requested was itself.

**What I did first.** I looked at the image, then read it pixel by pixel. Two things stood out:
- **A floor.** Every day has a solid row of dots at the very bottom.
- **Day-sized clumps.** Along that floor the dots form groups about 4.8 px apart. 4.8 px is 300 px divided by 61, so it is one day. Each group is a long run of hours at the same lowest value, and the gaps between groups are hours when the value was higher.

**How I got to the form.**
- **Recovering the values.** One pixel covers about 5 hours, so the values can't be read off directly. I wrote a model of how the dots were drawn (dot size, darkness, overlap, where each hour lands across the width). I then adjusted every hour's height until the redrawn picture matched the JPEG as closely as possible.
- **Night block.** I tested whether each day's floor hours form one block that lengthens slowly. That version fit as well as or better than leaving every hour free (fit error 105 against 113), so I kept it.
- **What the fit shows.**
  - The floor run grows from about 7½ to about 10½ hours per day across the 61 days.
  - The highest values each day drop by roughly 15% toward the end.
  - Some days are dull all day: the daily totals range from about 200 to 670 (in pixel-height units).
- **Why a spiral.** A spiral one day per turn lines up the daily floor and lets the slow change show as a shape.

**Things you should know.**
- **Hour order within a day is approximate.** Because a pixel covers about 5 hours, neighbouring hours can't be told apart in the picture. I chose their order for smoothness, swapping values only between hours at most 2 apart. The night wedge, how it widens, and the brightness of each day are well supported. The fine grain inside each turn is only partly real.
- **The text on the page assumes my reading of the data.** The caption says "noon at the top, midnight below". "Noon" there means the middle of each day's raised hours, not a known clock time. By the record's own count that middle falls around hour 16, so the record probably doesn't start at local midnight, or it is in a time standard offset from local solar time.
- **Scale.** Values are relative. 0 is the floor and 78 is the highest value I recovered, which is one isolated spike on about day 24. No units can be recovered.

**What I think was measured.** Sunlight at ground level, hourly: solar irradiance, or something driven by it such as solar-panel output. This rests on:
- **A hard zero every day.** The values sit at exactly the lowest level for a single block of hours each day. That looks like darkness, not a quantity that keeps varying overnight.
- **That block lengthens steadily** from about 7½ to 10½ hours, which matches nights getting longer.
- **Daytime peaks fall by about 15%,** as they would when the sun's midday height drops.
- **Clear days and cloudy days.** Some days rise to a dense cluster near the top (clear), and others stay low all day (overcast).

Daylight of roughly 16–17 hours shrinking to about 13½ fits a high latitude, around 50–60°, from about midsummer into late summer. That would be roughly late June to late August in the north, or the matching months in the south; the hemisphere can't be told from the picture.
