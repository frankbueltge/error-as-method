**The work is "Sixty-one Suns".** It's a still solargraph, like the long-exposure pinhole photo where the sun burns one arc across the sky each day. There is one arc per day, sunrise to sunset across a dark south-facing sky. The light along each arc is the measured value hour by hour, so clear days burn bright and cloudy hours go dim. The arcs step down and get narrower from the first day (top) to the last. The page is self-contained (canvas, data embedded inline, no external loads) and was checked in headless Chromium at 1100×800 with no errors.

**What I did first.** I looked at the image, then dumped its pixels as text. The bottom rows held a band of dense dot clusters, one per night when the value is zero. There were 61 of them, evenly spaced every 4.82 px with very little scatter, so the time axis is steady at about 0.2 px per hour. The data runs from about x=2.1 to x=295.9.

**From the file to the form.**
- **Getting values back.** At 0.2 px per hour, about five hourly dots share each pixel column, so they can't be read off directly. I built a model that draws dots (radius about 0.85 px, slightly transparent) and adjusted each of the 1,464 dot heights until the drawn image matched the JPEG best. A weak pull toward neighbouring hours helped decide which dot belongs to which hour inside a column.
- **Fitting the scale.** I set zero at the night band (y≈84) and called the highest dot (y≈6) 1.0.
- **Placing each arc.** The average daily curve gave solar noon, which splits the record into solar days. Counting lit hours per day gave a steady fall from about 15.7 to 13.1. I turned that day length into the sun's angle for each day at about 48° N, and that sets each arc's path.

**What you should know.**
- **Hourly values are approximate.** The fit is good overall, but which dot in a column belongs to which hour is partly a guess. Daily totals, peaks and the day-length trend are much more reliable than any single hour.
- **The arc positions use one outside fact.** The paths use 48° N, my own general knowledge of where north-eastern Montana is (nothing was looked up). Everything else that places the arcs comes from the file.
- **The value scale is relative.** There are no units or axes, so brightness only means "fraction of the highest value in the record".
- **The start position is uncertain.** Where the first hour sits horizontally is only pinned down by the left and right edges of the ink, give or take about half a pixel (2–3 hours). It doesn't change the picture, because noon is taken from the data itself.
- **The page carries a short caption** with the title, place and a one-line key. It's text in the page, not part of the drawing, if you want a version without words.

**My view of what was measured.** I think it is hourly global solar radiation on a level surface, as stated, over roughly July to August.
- **It's sunlight:** every day has about 8–9 consecutive hours at exactly zero. The non-zero hours form a smooth, symmetric daily hump, and some days are dim or broken, as clouds would make them.
- **It's high summer:** about 15.7 lit hours a day at the start only happens near midsummer at this latitude.
- **It's after midsummer:** the lit hours fall steadily to about 13.1. The clear-day peak holds steady (about 0.85–0.9 of the maximum) until around day 40, then drops to about 0.65–0.75 by the end.
- **Dating it:** at about 48° N those day lengths correspond to roughly early July and early September. That's an estimate, not a date. I couldn't tell the time zone of the timestamps, so I didn't guess one.
- **Weather:** clearly cloudy days include days 0, 2, 7, 23, 25 and 49, counted in solar days from the start.
