I made "Sixty-One Turns": one spiral of light on a dark ground, with one turn per day. The first day is at the centre and the last at the rim, midnight points down and noon points up. Each hour is a stretch of band whose width and warmth follow the value. I couldn't open the page in a browser, but I checked its script for errors and drew a close Python copy of it to judge the layout.

**What I did first**
I looked at the image, then printed its pixels as text and enlarged it 4×. The bottom row was the giveaway. It is a band of exactly 61 equal clusters about 4.82 px apart, which is one per 24 hours. Between the clusters the dots rise and fall back every day. Enlarged, the dots on clear days line up in horizontal rows: the same hour reaches nearly the same height day after day.

**From the file to the form**
- **Finding the dots:** I located every dot in the picture (719 of them; 637 sit above the floor).
- **Fixing the time scale:** I fitted the timing of the night clusters to get about 0.2009 px per hour. The first point is at x≈3.6, and the first night's centre is about 1.2 hours in.
- **Rebuilding the hours:** I turned the dots back into 1,464 hourly values. Night hours are set to zero. On each day, the dots before noon are given out in rising order and those after noon in falling order.
- **Checking it:** I re-drew this series in the style of the original, and it matches it closely.
- **The page:** the series is embedded in `index.html` and drawn on a canvas. It loads nothing from outside.

**What you should know**
- **The order within each day is reconstructed.** Dots overlap at this resolution, so I can't recover which hour each daytime value belongs to. The night lengths, daily peaks and cloudy days are faithful to the picture; the morning and afternoon order is my reconstruction, and the note says so.
- **The value scale is relative.** The picture has no numbers, so values run from 0 to the record's maximum.

**What I think was measured**
Global (incoming) solar radiation, recorded hourly, from roughly mid-July to mid-September. The season is a rough estimate (within a few weeks). It rests on these points:
1. Every night the value sits on exactly the same floor, about 8–14 hours a day. That is a hard zero, not just a low reading.
2. Daytime values rise and fall once a day, and on clear days repeat at the same heights from day to day, as the sun's position would produce.
3. Some days are dim and scattered, as clouds would make them.
4. The night stretch widens from about 3 px to 4 px across the record. The hours above zero drop from about 15–17 a day to about 9–12.
5. Clear-day peaks fall about 15% (roughly 70 to 59 in relative units) between day 12 and day 58.

At about 48°N, points 4 and 5 fit late summer into early autumn better than early summer. The nights fall in the same place each day, so the hours look like local time starting near midnight, but that is uncertain.
