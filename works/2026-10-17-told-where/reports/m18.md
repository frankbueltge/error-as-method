I made the work and called it **Sixty-One Days Exposure**. It's a single canvas image of 61 nested arcs of light over a dark horizon, like a solargraph (a pinhole photo left open for weeks so the sun burns one arc per day). The page and its note are `/tmp/claude-0/s133/mk/m18/work/index.html` and `/tmp/claude-0/s133/mk/m18/work/NOTE.md`. The page stands alone with the data built in, and it made no network requests when I rendered it headlessly at 1100×800.

**First step with the file.** I looked at the picture, then turned its darkness into ASCII text, one character per pixel, to see how the dots are laid out. The night-time zeros make a strip of dark blocks along the bottom, one per night, with a small gap at each midday. Those gaps acted as a built-in clock.

**From the file to the form.**
1. I found the dot centres by looking for the darkest point in each small patch of pixels, which gave about 730 dots (Python, scripts in `/tmp/claude-0/s133/mk/m18/scratch`).
2. I fixed the midday positions from the gaps in the bottom strip, about 4.83 px apart. Each dot went to its nearest midday, with its hour counted from noon and its value read as height above the bottom strip.
3. For each day I joined those points into a curve of light through the day.
4. Each day became a sun-path arc using the standard formula for how high the sun stands at each hour. The brightness and thickness of the arc at each hour come from the value measured then.
5. Arcs go downward in day order, and the lit part of each arc gets shorter, following trends I fitted to the data.

**What you should know.**
- **The values are read back from a small JPEG, not from the original numbers.** One pixel column holds about 5 hours, and many dots overlap. I recovered roughly 10–14 of the 24 hours per day, and the hour of each is only accurate to about ±1–2 hours. The curve between those points is filled in by straight-line joins, so the fine flicker along an arc is partly a side effect of that, not clouds.
- **The spacing between arcs is exaggerated so each day can be seen.** In the data the highest midday values fall only about 19%. Drawn to scale, the 61 arcs would sit about 2 px apart and blur together, so I spread them evenly instead. The NOTE says only that the arcs go down in the order of the days and why. It doesn't mention that the spacing is exaggerated, so add that if it matters.
- **The record doesn't line up with midnight.** It runs across 62 middays, starting about 6 hours before the first noon and ending a few hours after the last. The first and last arcs are therefore drawn only in part.
- **No text on the image.** The original has none, and I kept it that way.

**What I think was measured.** Hourly sunlight on a level surface, taken over about two months just after midsummer at roughly 45–55° latitude. That view rests on four things in the file, and it is a hypothesis:
- **Long days:** the light lasts about 13–15 hours, with clear zeros at night.
- **Falling midday peaks:** the best middays drop by about 19% from start to end. That fits the noon sun going from about 63° to 49° high at around 50° latitude.
- **Shortening days:** the lit part of each day gets shorter by roughly 2–3 hours over the 61 days.
- **Varied weather:** most days are fairly clear, with a few heavily overcast ones (about days 1, 3, 8, 26 and 50, counting from 0).

That would be about July–August in the north or January–February in the south; the file can't tell which. If the timestamps are UTC starting at midnight, local noon falls at about 05:00–06:00 UTC. That would put the place near 90–100°E, but this is a weak guess.
