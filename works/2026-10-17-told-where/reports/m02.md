I made **Sun Print**. It shows the 61 days as a cyanotype, the old photographic print that sunlight itself turns blue. Each row is one day and each column is one hour, with solar noon in the middle. The more light an hour got, the deeper its blue. Night is left as bare paper. A thin strip on the right shows each day's total. I opened the page in headless Chromium at 1100 × 800 and checked the screenshot: the only file it loads is itself, and there are no errors.

**What I did first with the file.** I read the JPEG's pixels and printed a darkness map. Along the bottom there is a dense band of dots at zero (night), broken by short gaps (daytime) that repeat every 4.82 px. I measured 60 gap centres and fitted a straight line to them; points stay within about 0.1 px of it. That fixed the spacing at 0.201 px per hour and showed where each day's light is centred: about hour 17 of each 24-hour block.

**How I got from the file to the form.**
- **Recovering the values.** I rebuilt all 1,464 values by drawing a model dot at each hour's known x position. For each dot I tried every height and kept the one that best reproduced the picture, repeating until the result stopped changing.
- **What can be trusted.** Each dot is about 1.5 px wide, which spans about seven hours. So the overall shape of each day, the day's peak, its total, and the night lengths come back reliably. Which value belongs to which exact hour does not; neighbouring hours can come back swapped.
- **What I did about that.** I smoothed the values over about one hour, and the print's soft, brushed edges are deliberate so it doesn't claim more precision than the source has.
- **Fitting the days into rows.** I cut the series into rows running from one solar midnight to the next. That drops the first 5 values, which are night. It also leaves the last row cut off mid-afternoon (the hard edge at bottom right), because that is where the record stops.

**Things you should know.**
- The values are read back from a small compressed image, not taken from the original data. There are no units: the scale is height in pixels of the original, with 78 as the highest.
- The thin text on the sheet (title, caption, day numbers, noon/midnight marks) is my addition.

**What I think was measured, with the evidence.**
- **The days are getting shorter.** The zero band at night gets steadily longer: 2.9 px per night in the first ten days, 3.4 px in the last ten. That is roughly 15–16 hours of measurable light at the start and about 13 at the end. The night lengthens faster near the end, which is what happens as an equinox approaches.
- **The sun is getting lower.** Clear-day peaks fall from about 72–78 to about 57–62, roughly 0.8 times as high.
- **Season and place.** Together these look like a mid-latitude site (about 40–55°) going from high summer toward the autumn equinox. In the northern hemisphere that would be roughly late July to late September; in the southern, late January to late March. The picture can't say which hemisphere.
- **Clock.** The light is centred about 17 hours after the first sample. If the timestamps are UTC starting at midnight, that would put the site in the longitudes of eastern North America. That is a guess about how the times were recorded, not a finding.
- **Weather.** Overcast days are clear to see (about rows 1, 8, 26, 50), and many afternoons look broken up, as if by passing cloud.
