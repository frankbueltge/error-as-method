I made "Light Rings, Fort Peck": the 61 days of hourly values wound into a spiral, one turn per day. It is in /tmp/claude-0/s133/mk/m12/work/index.html (about 18 KB, loads nothing outside itself), with the note in /tmp/claude-0/s133/mk/m12/work/NOTE.md. Working scripts and test screenshots are in /tmp/claude-0/s133/mk/m12/scratch. I rendered the page in headless Chromium at 1100 × 800: no errors, no network requests.

**What I did first.** I looked at the image, then wrote out its pixels as text characters so I could read the layout. Two things stood out:
- A dark band along the bottom edge that breaks into short clusters at a steady rhythm. Every day, many values sit at exactly the same minimum, which is the lowest level in the plot.
- The image is only 300 px wide for 1,464 values, so one pixel column holds about 4.9 hours. You cannot read individual values in time order directly.

**How I got to the form.**
- **Finding the dots.** I found the dots as points of local darkness, with positions finer than a pixel. Where a dot was darker than a single dot should be, I counted it as two or more overlapping values. Height above the floor became the value: 0 at the floor, 1 at the highest dot.
- **Splitting into days.** I fitted the floor rhythm: one cycle every 4.82 px, which is 24 hours. I split the record at the middle of each floor cluster, so each turn of the spiral runs midnight to midnight with noon at the top. From the width of each floor cluster I estimated where each day's zero hours start and end.
- **Drawing each turn.** Along each turn, the colour comes from a smoothed blend of the dots I found, plus the zero hours. Each turn is drawn as a single stroke, with colour changing round the circle, so there are no seams.
- **Showing the source.** Small pale points mark the dots exactly where I found them.

The spiral makes the slow changes visible: the lit fan narrows and the zero-hours (blue thread) widen toward the outside, the noon glow cools, and a few dim days show up as dark rings.

**What you should know.**
- **Values are relative.** There are no units and no absolute scale. The single brightest value is set to 1.
- **The order of hours within a day is approximate.** Because about 5 hours share each pixel column, a value's position on the turn could be off by roughly 1–2 hours. The smoothing hides this, but the shape of each day is a reconstruction, not a reading.
- **Some low values are lost.** Small values near dawn and dusk run into the floor band and are partly missing. Where dots overlap, how many there are is only estimated from how dark they are.
- **The first and last days are incomplete**, because the record starts and ends partway through a day. The innermost turn is only a fragment, and the outermost one stops in the afternoon.

**What I think was measured: incoming sunlight (solar radiation at ground level), over midsummer into late summer.** This is my reading, not something the picture states. It rests on:
- **Night zeros.** Every day the record drops to exactly the same floor for several hours, and in between rises in a hump with values scattered at every height. Sunlight behaves like this. Temperature, wind or humidity would not sit at an identical floor every night.
- **Shrinking days.** The zero stretch widens over the record, from about 6 hours a day at the start to about 9 at the end. The days are getting shorter.
- **Lower peaks.** Clear-day peaks fall gently, from about 0.92 to about 0.76.
- **Season.** Short nights getting longer and a modest drop in peaks fit roughly late June to late August at Fort Peck's latitude better than autumn. In autumn the nights would be much longer and the peaks would fall more steeply. This is the least certain part.
- **Cloudy days.** Dim days scattered through the record look like overcast days. One value, about a third of the way in, sits clearly above every other peak, which fits a brief burst of extra light at the edge of a cloud.
- **Probably UTC timestamps (weak).** The middle of each night falls about 6–7 hours after each day's boundary in the record. That fits timestamps in UTC rather than Montana local time.
