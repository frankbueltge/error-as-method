I made a work called **Sixty-One Turns**. All 1,464 hourly values are wound into one spiral, one turn per day, and the brightness along it is the sunlight. It is a single canvas image with the data built into the page and nothing loaded from outside. No browser was available, so I checked it with a render in node, which leaves out the text column. Nobody has opened `index.html` in a real browser yet.

**What I did first:** I looked at the picture, then printed every pixel as text to read it directly. I found three things:
- Each dot is about 2 px across.
- The zero line sits at about y = 83.5, and the plot's top is at about y = 2.
- There are only about 0.2 px per hour, so the five or so values in each pixel column overlap sideways.

**From the file to the form:**
- **Spacing and position.** A periodogram of the dark band along the zero line gives a period of 4.824 px per day, which is 0.2010 px per hour. Dots above the zero line at both edges put the first value at about x = 2.6.
- **Recovering the values.** I rebuilt the hourly values by fitting a model of the plot to the image. Each value is a soft dot, and each value's height is adjusted in turn to reduce the difference from the JPEG, with a weak pull towards its neighbours. This is a reconstruction, not a reading.
  - Daily peaks, day length and how clouds vary from day to day are reliable.
  - The order of hours within a few-hour stretch, and the small morning and evening values, are only approximate. The page says the values are approximate.
- **The form.** The disc is laid out like a map seen from above, with north at the top. Each hour is placed where the sun would stand at that hour, so noon is at the bottom (south), afternoon on the west side, and night is a dark wedge at the top.
  - I didn't know the clock time, so I lined up "noon" with the centre of the light in the data.
  - The disc echoes the instrument itself, a level disc facing the sky.
  - The spiral shows that the days are consecutive and also repeat.

**What you should know:**
- There is a small text column: a title, a few sentences of explanation (including that Fort Peck is in Montana) and a colour key. There are faint compass letters (N, E, S, W).
- In the recovered values the morning side looks wider than the evening side. That may be a fitting artefact rather than something real.

**What I think was measured:** total sunlight on a level surface, probably at a summer station of the US SURFRAD network ("Fort Peck, 634 m" matches one), with times in UTC. Over the 61 days the days get shorter, so the stretch is most likely just after the summer solstice, roughly late June to the end of August. My confidence is moderate on summer and lower on the exact months. This rests on:
- **Night length:** only about 8 to 10 hours a night are at zero, so about 14 to 15 hours have light. At about 48°N that means summer.
- **Shortening days:** the gaps between night bands on the zero line close up from left to right. In the raw pixels they read "### +##+" at the start and "####" at the end, so nights get longer through the record. In the fit, the last evening hours with light also drop out.
- **Lower noons:** the highest values fall a little, from about 65–75 to about 50–60 on the plot's scale. That fits a noon sun getting lower.
- **UTC:** the series starts and ends in daylight. The centre of the light falls about 19.3 hours after the first value, which matches solar noon in UTC at roughly 106°W, if the series starts at 00 UTC.
- **Weather:** many days are broken and uneven, which looks like summer cumulus and storms.

I can't tell the measurement units from the picture.
