**What I did first.** I looked at the picture, then printed its 300 × 90 pixels as a text grid so I could read it pixel by pixel. Three things stood out:
- **A thick dark band at rows 83–84.** It is the run of zero readings at night, broken by short gaps every 4.8 px or so, which are the daylight hours.
- **A few faint dots just below that band.** These are slightly negative readings.
- **A top edge that falls from left to right.**

**How I got to the form.**
1. **Day length in pixels.** A frequency fit on the zero band gives a period of 4.822 px. That matches 1,464 values spread over about 294 px, so 24 hours is about 4.8 px.
2. **Cutting the strip into days.** I split it from the middle of one night to the middle of the next. For each day I measured the width of the daylight gap in the zero band, the highest dot, and the total ink above zero.
3. **Extracting the dots.** I found 774 dots, each with a sub-pixel position and a darkness weight: 759 above zero and 15 below.
4. **The wheel.** Time is bent into a circle: the 61 days run clockwise from an opening at the top, where the record starts and ends. Zero becomes an inner ring, so each night is one blue piece of that ring and each dawn is a small break between pieces. Every dot keeps its own time position and height, so its distance from the ring is how much light there was. Colour runs from ember at low values to white near the maximum, and the below-zero readings are faint blue specks inside the ring. Faint spokes at each night's centre and small ticks at each noon let you count the days.

**What you should know:**
- **Hours can't be read from this file.** One pixel covers about 5 hours, so the time of day of a given value is not recoverable. I didn't invent hourly values; every mark comes from a dot or from the darkness of the night band as measured. A day therefore shows as a spray of light rather than a smooth arc.
- **Some readings are merged.** Overlapping dots in the JPEG run together, so the 759 dots above zero slightly undercount the roughly 800 daytime readings.
- **The picture has no units.** All heights are relative to the largest value in the picture.
- **Words on the page.** It has a title, one line about the place, and a three-line key in small type. The rest is image.

**My view of what was measured.** I think it is global solar radiation on a horizontal surface, recorded by an instrument whose readings dip slightly below zero at night, covering about late June to late August of one summer. That is an inference; it rests on these things in the file:
- **The instrument.** Readings just below zero at night are typical of thermopile instruments that measure sunlight, which is real behaviour rather than plotting noise.
- **Day length.** The daylight gap stays at about 1.85 px for the first ~23 days, then narrows faster, to about 1.3 px. That is roughly 2.5–3 fewer hours of daylight. A flat stretch followed by a speeding-up decline is how day length behaves in the weeks after the summer solstice at about 48°N.
- **Noon peaks.** On clear days the peaks drop to about 0.82–0.86 of their early height. That fits the noon sun sinking from about 65° to about 50° above the horizon between late June and late August. A September–October window would show a steeper fall, to about 0.7.
- **One spike on about day 25.** It stands well above its neighbours and is likely cloud enhancement, when sunlight reflecting off nearby clouds briefly pushes a reading above clear sky. That is common on summer days with scattered clouds.

A weaker point: the first night centre falls about 5–6 hours after the first reading, which suggests the timestamps are in UTC. That one is uncertain to a couple of hours.

Fort Peck at 634 m matches a US radiation monitoring station I already knew of. I did not look anything up.
