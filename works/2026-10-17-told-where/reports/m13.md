I made **Sixty-One Turns**. Each day is one ring, the first innermost and the last outermost, with midday at the top and midnight at the bottom. The colour at each point shows that hour's value, from dark through ember to near-white. Nothing draws the horizon: the dark wedge at the bottom is just where the values fall to nothing each night. The outer ring stops in the afternoon, where the record ends. I rendered the page in a headless browser at 1100 × 800: it shows correctly and loads no other file.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at the picture and then printed its pixels as text (300 × 90). The bottom edge was the key. Below all the scattered dots there is a flat, very dark band at row 83–84, and it is broken by regular light gaps. I measured the spacing of those gaps at 4.82 px, with very little drift (about 0.2 px). With 61 days across the width, that is one gap per day. So the quantity sits at the same minimum value for part of every day, and it only rises in the hours the gaps mark.

**How I got from the file to the form.**
- **Reading the plot's layout.** The gap spacing gives 0.2009 px per hour, which leaves a margin of about 3 px on each side. The minimum line sits at y = 84 and the highest dot at y ≈ 6. A lone dot has a radius of about 0.6–0.8 px.
- **Rebuilding the hourly values.** About five hours fall into each pixel column, so the values can't simply be read off. I modelled each dot as a small anti-aliased disc at its known x position and searched for the 1,464 heights whose rendering best matches the JPEG. I added a slight preference for smooth change from hour to hour, because neighbouring hours can swap places with almost no effect on the image.
- **Building the page.** The rebuilt values (0–100, relative to the record's maximum) are embedded in the page, and plain JavaScript draws them onto a canvas.

**What you should know.**
- **The hourly values are an estimate, not a reading.** The overall shape is reliable: which hours are lit, how high each day's values go, and which days are dull. The exact order of hours within each pixel column is partly guessed. That shows as grain in the rings, plus a few stray specks of light in night hours.
- **The text on the page names Fort Peck and the altitude.** That comes from your brief, not from the image.

**My view of what was measured: incoming sunlight (solar irradiance), probably hourly averages, recorded in early-to-mid summer.** It rests on these points:
1. Every day has a long stretch at exactly the same floor value with nothing below it, which is what you'd see if the quantity is zero at night.
2. The rise each day is smooth and single-peaked, and some days are much lower or ragged, which looks like cloud.
3. The light lasts about 15–16 hours and the night only about 8, which at 48°N points to around the summer solstice.
4. If the hour count starts at midnight UTC, the centre of each day's light falls at about hour 18–19. That matches solar noon at roughly 105°W, where Montana is.
5. The daily peaks drift down by roughly 15–20% from the first day to the last. That suggests the record runs from around the solstice into late summer, though haze could also do it.
6. The single highest value is an isolated spike, which looks like the brief brightening that happens at the edge of a cloud.

One thing comes from my own memory rather than the file: I recall that Fort Peck is a ground station that measures surface solar radiation. I looked nothing up.
