I made "Gathered Light", a still image of 62 stacked ridges of warm light, one for each day in the record. It is in `/tmp/claude-0/s133/mk/m05/work/index.html`, with `/tmp/claude-0/s133/mk/m05/work/NOTE.md` beside it. The page is a single inline SVG drawn at 1100×800 (about 600 KB) and loads nothing else. No browser was available here, so I checked it with an approximate preview drawn in Python, without the text, and confirmed the SVG is valid XML. It has not been seen in a real browser.

**What I did first.** I decoded the JPEG to grayscale and printed it as a character grid so I could read it pixel by pixel. That showed a dense band of dots along the bottom (rows 83–84) broken into regular blocks, about 61 of them, and ragged columns of dots above.

**From the file to the form.**
- **Daily rhythm:** fitting a repeating pattern to the image gave a period of 4.82 px, which is one day. The dots sit high exactly halfway between the floor blocks, and folding all days onto one gives an arch: floor at night, highest midway, in between on either side.
- **Dot size:** I measured single isolated dots (about 2 px across) to turn the amount of ink into a count of hours.
- **Half-days:** for each half-day (night to noon, noon to night) I counted how many hours reached each height. The individual hours cannot be put back in order, because about five dots overlap in every pixel column. So inside each half-day I laid the hours out from noon outward, strongest nearest.
- **The ridges:** each ridge is as wide as the hours that day spent above the floor, and its outline shows how long it stayed at each level. Within a half-day that rearrangement is the only liberty taken. Ridges run from the first day at the top to the last at the bottom. Nights are cool flat lines, and the light is coloured by height, from dark ember to pale near-white.

**What you should know.**
- **Accuracy:** every value is an estimate from a 300×90 compressed image. Overlapping dots are under-counted, compression noise was removed with a fixed threshold, and the ridges are lightly smoothed. The work keeps the overall shape of the record, not exact numbers.
- **Why 62 ridges for 61 days:** the 1,464 hours cover 61 nights. Counted noon to noon, that makes 60 full days plus a fragment at each end, and both fragments are drawn as they are.
- **Ridge shape:** the order of hours within each half-day is a rearrangement, not a recovered time series. NOTE.md and the caption in the work both say so.

**What I think was measured.** Very likely sunlight reaching the ground (solar irradiance), or something driven by it such as solar-panel output, in summer and moving away from midsummer. This rests on:
- **A flat floor every night**, shared by about 9–11 hours a day.
- **A smooth daily arch** peaking midway between floors, with the morning and afternoon nearly symmetric once noon is placed correctly.
- **Variation from day to day:** clear days reach the top while others stay low, which looks like cloud.
- **A slow decline:** the highest point of clear days falls about 15% across the record, and time above the floor shrinks from about 15 to about 12.5 hours a day. That points to days getting shorter after the summer solstice, roughly mid-June to mid-August in the north or the matching months in the south. I can't tell which hemisphere.
- **One spike:** on about day 25 the record's highest value appears on an otherwise dim day. Brief jumps above clear-sky level like this are typical at broken cloud edges.

One weaker inference: the record seems to begin around the middle of a (solar) day rather than at midnight. If the data starts at a clock midnight, its timestamps are probably not local solar time, for example UTC at a site far from Greenwich. I would not lean on this.
