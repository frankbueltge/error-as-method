I made "The Light Draws In": the record is cut at each local midnight and the days are stacked as 62 glowing lines, with noon at the centre. Reading down, the band of light narrows and cools from late summer into autumn. I couldn't screenshot the finished page because there's no browser here.

**What I did first.** I looked at the image, then dumped it pixel by pixel as a text grid to see its structure. The zero values ("night") form a thick dark line at rows 83–84. It breaks every 4.82 px, and each break is a day. I fitted that period: 60 full breaks plus a partial one at each edge. I then located 716 dots, 648 of them above the zero line, with sub-pixel centres. Height was scaled so that 0 is the zero line and 1 is near the top of the image, so all values are relative; there is no absolute scale.

**From the file to the form.** Each dot got a day and a sun-time hour from its x position relative to the fitted noons. Within each day I sorted the lit dots by x and placed them on consecutive hours around that day's centre. Each day then became a smoothly interpolated line of colour from night indigo through ember and amber to pale noon. On the canvas, a right-hand column shows each day's summed light. A strip along the bottom redraws the original 1,464-hour record with faint rules where it was cut.

**What you should know:**
- **Hour order inside a day is reconstructed, not read.** One pixel holds about 5 hours, so the order of dots within a day comes from noisy x positions, and dots that overlap are lost. Treat the texture inside a line as approximate. The robust parts are the lit width, the peak brightness and the daily sum.
- **The first and last lines are partial.** Hours outside the record are left blank with a dotted mark. Sun time is used, not clock time.
- **The faint marks just under the zero line are probably compression artefacts, not negative readings.** They sit in rows 85–87, which fall in the same 8-pixel JPEG block as the heavy zero line. They could instead be the small negative night readings typical of such sensors, but I left them out.
- **One bright spike** near day 24, at the top of the image, is shown only as that day's brightest moment.

**What I think was measured, and why.** It's global horizontal solar radiation (all sunlight reaching a level surface), sampled hourly:
- There is a clean zero every night and a 24-hour rhythm.
- Days are ragged and broken by cloud; some days stay low all day.
- The record's clock is very likely UTC. Fitting the noons puts the first hour at about 17:00 sun time and the last at about 16:00. That is exactly what a 00:00–23:00 UTC record looks like at roughly 106°W. A local-time record would start and end in darkness.

**When.** My best guess is late summer into early autumn, somewhere between about mid-July and late September:
- The lit span per day shrinks by about 3 hours over the 61 days (about 12–13 lit hours early, 9–10 late).
- The clear-day peak falls to about 80% of its starting height.
- Both point to shortening days and a lowering sun after the solstice, at about 48°N.

The pixel resolution doesn't allow a narrower date.
