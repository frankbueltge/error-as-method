# Sixty-one Suns

The work redraws the record as a solargraph, the long-exposure pinhole photograph in which the sun burns one arc across the sky for every day. Each of the 61 days (plus the part-days at either end) becomes an arc running from sunrise to sunset across a south-facing sky. The light along the arc is that day's measured value, hour by hour, so clear days burn bright and unbroken and cloudy hours go dim. The arcs are placed using nothing but the record. Solar noon comes from the average daily curve. The height and width of each arc come from the number of lit hours per day, which falls steadily from about 15.7 to about 13.1 across the record. That count gives the sun's declination for each day at roughly 48° N, so the first day is the highest and widest arc and the last is the lowest. I chose this form because the record is sunlight measured on the ground. A solargraph is the picture the same sun makes of itself in that sky over the same weeks, and it shows the two things the dot plot hides: each day as a whole, and the season slowly bringing the sun lower.
