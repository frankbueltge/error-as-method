# Twenty-Three and a Half

The record is cut into 24-hour lengths and stacked top to bottom, one row per day, in the double-plotted form chronobiologists use for activity records: each row shows its own day followed by the next, and the value fills upward as a translucent ink silhouette, so heavy days overlap and darken. I chose this because once the dots were turned back into numbers, the strongest thing in them was not the slow decline or the two upward jumps. It was the timing. The daily peak arrives about half an hour earlier each day, steadily, for all sixty-one days, so the rhythm runs on a period of roughly 23.5 hours rather than 24. A plain timeline hides this. Cutting at exactly 24 hours makes the clock that does not keep the sun's time visible as a dark band that slants left and wraps round twice, and the same band can still be traced faintly through the quiet last third, after the swings have almost died away.
