I finished the work. It's called "Sixty-One Pulses" and is in `/tmp/claude-0/s137/mk/m02/work/index.html`, with `NOTE.md` next to it. My working scripts are in `/tmp/claude-0/s137/mk/m02/scratch/`.

**First steps with the file.** I looked at the picture, then printed its darkness as a text map and measured how far the dark pixels reached in each column. That showed the main feature right away. The dots fill about 296 pixels across, so one day is about 4.9 pixels wide. Across the first two-thirds of the record, every 4.9 pixels there is a sawtooth: the value jumps up once a day and then falls steadily until the next jump.

**From the file to the form.**
- I found each dot's centre (about 1,155 separate dots; the rest overlap) and put them in time order. Where I couldn't tell the order of dots in the same column, I assumed the sawtooth: the value falls during the day and the jump comes last.
- From those dots I rebuilt 1,464 approximate values and embedded them in the page.
- The page draws one column per day (61 in all) and one row per hour (24, counted from the first reading). A dot's size and darkness give the value, so each day reads as a pulse that thins out.
- Under the grid, the same readings run end to end as one continuous line.
- It is all inline SVG drawn by plain JavaScript, with no external resources and no animation, sized for 1100 × 800.

**What the commissioner should know.**
- **The values are approximate.** They were traced out of a 300 × 90 compressed image where many dots overlap. Exact heights and the hour order within each day are guesses, and the bottom line looks more jagged than the real record probably is. The page says this in a small caption.
- **The hour rows don't line up with the real clock.** Row 1 is just the first reading, because I don't know when the record started. As a result, some daily jumps fall across the boundary between two columns.
- **I couldn't check it in a real browser.** None was available, so I checked the layout by redrawing the page's shapes with Python. Fonts and text size in the actual page may differ a little from that preview.

**What I think was measured.** I'm not certain, but my best guess is the flow or water level of a stream fed by melting snow, near the end of the melt season. This rests on:
- **The daily shape:** a sharp rise once a day followed by a long, slow fall. That is the classic daily pattern of meltwater flow.
- **Days 9–10:** there is no pulse at all for about two days and the baseline sinks. That fits a cold or cloudy spell that stopped the melt.
- **Around day 27:** a single large surge, much bigger than the rest. That fits a heavy rain event.
- **Days 28–43:** the daily pulse gets smaller day by day, and after about day 43 the readings are low and nearly flat with only tiny daily bumps. That fits a snowpack running out.

The main alternative is soil moisture under daily automatic watering. I rate it lower, because watering would make jumps of about the same size every day and an abrupt stop. Here the jumps fade gradually, and two whole days are missing.
