I've made the work: a single static SVG page called "Sixty-One Wicks", with a NOTE.md beside it. The page loads nothing else; its only URL is the standard SVG namespace declaration, which fetches nothing. One thing I could not do: no tool here renders SVG with gradients, so I have not seen the page in a browser. I checked the composition on a PIL drawing of the same shapes, which did not include the text.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at it, then read the pixels column by column with Python/PIL. The dots run across columns 2 to 297, which is about 4.85 px per day. The sharp rises repeat about every 4.9 px, so the main structure is a daily cycle.

**How I got from the file to the form.**
- **Rebuilding the hours:** I grouped the dark pixels in each column into dots, ordered them through each fall and rise, and resampled to 1,464 hourly values scaled 0 to 1.
- **The picture this gave:** in a typical day the value climbs for about 6–8 hours and falls for about 16. The daily swing narrows through November. 24–25 October are low, flat days. 10–11 November jump high, and that night's minimum stays high. From about 27 November (day 43) to the end, the series is low and nearly flat, with only a faint trace of the daily dip.
- **The form:** each day is one upright column, read downward through its 24 hours. Width and colour (slate to ember to pale gold) both follow the value, so each day becomes a flame-like shape that pinches at the same hour every day. Through October the columns glow, through November they dim, and the last 18 are grey threads. A thin line at the foot shows the same 1,464 hours unfolded, so the source stays in view.

**What the commissioner should know.**
- **The hourly values are approximate.** At about 5 values per pixel column, with JPEG blur, individual hours can't be read exactly; within a column they are smoothed, interpolated estimates. The day-scale shapes, the trend, and the dates of the events are reliable.
- **The hour within each day is uncertain.** I assumed the first value is hour 0 of 15 October, which the file does not confirm. Columns break at the recorded day boundaries; nothing is shifted to a clock.
- **The page itself:** the title, a one-line key and four date marks are the only text. It scales to fit the window and is laid out for 1100 × 800.

**My view of what was measured.** I think it is most likely a temperature taken near the surface, outdoors or in somewhere sun-exposed, at a northern mid- or high-latitude site, from autumn into early winter. Several things in the file point that way:
- a steady 24-hour cycle that rises over roughly a third of the day and falls over the rest, the usual heat-up-by-day, cool-by-night shape;
- a downward drift and a shrinking daily swing from October into December;
- low, flat days (24–25 October), which look like overcast cold spells;
- one warm event (10–11 November) whose night-time minimum stays high, which looks like a mild, cloudy air mass;
- after 27 November, an almost flat, low signal with a faint daily trace. That fits a sensor under snow (held near 0 °C) or a long cold, overcast spell.

If the recorded hours are clock time, the timing of the lows and highs suggests a timezone offset (UTC timestamps far from the site, perhaps East Asia), but I hold that loosely. A solar-charged battery voltage would fit the shape fairly well too, so I would call temperature a judgement, not a certainty.
