**What I did first with the file.** I looked at it, then turned it into a grid of darkness values (an ASCII dump and per-column dark extents) to read its structure.
- The dots run from x≈3 to 297, so one pixel column holds about 5 hours.
- Repeated autocorrelation and folding confirmed a 24-hour cycle that is very regular and lopsided. It rises fast, over about 5 hours, then falls slowly over 15–19 hours.
- Over the 61 days the record has:
  - **Days 1–42:** strong daily teeth.
  - **Two quiet spells:** days 9–11 and 24–26, where the level and the swing both drop.
  - **One surge:** day 27, which reaches the highest value in the record. Day 28's lowest point stays high, then levels ease back down.
  - **Days 27–42:** the level and the daily swing fall away steadily.
  - **Days 43–61:** the daily cycle almost disappears, leaving a low level that drifts down slowly with small wiggles.

**How I got to the form.**
- **Rebuilding the hourly values.** I first tried fitting 1,464 values so that redrawing them would match the picture. That fit the dots but scrambled their order within each column. What worked was a simpler pass: within each column, step through the dark pixels from the end nearest the previous column, and place the column's hours at evenly spaced points along that ink.
- **Checking it.** The rebuilt series has a clean 24-hour period and lines up with the original when redrawn.
- **Choosing the form.** A first version stacked one line per day; it read as wood grain and lost the rhythm. The spiral works better because every turn is exactly one day, so the same hour always lands at the same angle. The daily peak becomes a dark band in the upper left of the disc, with a pale crescent opposite it. The quiet spells show as pale rings, the surge as one heavy ring, and the final weeks as hairlines thinning toward an empty centre. The disc shape and the title play on the file's name, "record".

**What the commissioner should know.**
- The values are re-traced from a small, compressed image, so they are approximate. Daily structure and overall level are reliable, but individual hours are not. The order of readings within each 5-hour column is partly inferred.
- I don't know the clock time, so the spiral starts at whatever hour the record starts, at twelve o'clock. The angle of the dark band is relative to that start, not to midnight.
- The only text on the image is the title and a one-line caption.

**My view of what was measured (a guess).** My best guess is the flow or water level of a stream fed by snow or glacier melt, recorded from late summer into autumn. It rests on four things in the file:
- The daily shape is a fast rise and a long overnight decline, which is how melt pulses usually look.
- On the quiet spells the level and the swing both drop together, as if melt had shut down in cold weather.
- The day-27 surge looks like a storm: a sharp spike and a raised low point the next day, followed by a gradual decline over the following days.
- The swing collapses in the last three weeks to a slowly falling base, as if melt had ended.

The main alternative is a temperature series, such as air or near-surface temperature, which has a similar daily shape and autumn decline. If so, the abrupt loss of the daily cycle after day 42 might mean the sensor was buried under snow. I can't rule that out from the picture alone.
