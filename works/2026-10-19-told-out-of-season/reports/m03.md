I made a single still image called **Twenty-Three and a Half**. It cuts the 61-day record into 24-hour lengths and stacks them, which shows that the record has a daily rhythm whose peak comes about half an hour earlier every day. I had no browser here, so I never saw the page itself in one. I checked a raster copy drawn with the same geometry, without the curve smoothing and without the text.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at it, then printed its pixels as text to read the dots column by column. It looked like a sawtooth: a daily up-and-down whose overall level slowly falls, with two sudden jumps upward, and then a long, low, almost flat stretch.

**From the file to the form.**
- **Turning dots back into values:** 1,464 values across 300 pixels is about 4.9 values per column. For each column I worked out how many hours fall in it (4 or 5). I spread that many points over the column's darkness so that each point took an equal share of the ink. I then chose whether each column runs upward or downward so that it joins its neighbours smoothly. Three columns were blank and I filled them from their neighbours.
- **What the numbers showed:** I tested how strongly cycles of different lengths fit the series. The best fit is a cycle of about 23.5 hours (8,457), against about 1,450 for exactly 24 hours, about 1,200 for the 24.8-hour lunar day and almost nothing for 12-hour tidal cycles. Measured day by day, the hour of the daily peak moves steadily earlier, about 0.46 hours a day, from roughly hour 17 to roughly hour 23 of the day before. That is a drift of about 18 hours over 40 days. The small cycle left in the quiet final third keeps drifting at the same rate.
- **The form:** the page cuts the record every 24 hours and stacks the days from top to bottom. Each row shows that day followed by the next, the way activity records are laid out in circadian research. The value fills upward as translucent ink, so where days overlap the ink darkens. The decline, the jumps on days 11 and 27, and the collapse after about day 42 all show, but the main thing you see is the dark band of peaks slanting left and wrapping round twice. A small mark at the top and bottom shows where each row's two days meet. The only words on the page are the title and a one-line caption.

**What you should know.**
- **The numbers are estimates:** a 300×90 JPEG cannot give back the real values. They are only scaled from 0 to 100, which is the picture's height, so they have no units.
- **Detail finer than five hours is guessed:** the order of the 4–5 points inside each column is inferred, so the shape of each day's curve is approximate. Anything that happens over a day or longer, including the drift, comes from the columns themselves and is robust.
- **The drift depends on the samples being exactly hourly:** if the spacing were slightly off, the 23.5-hour period would really be a 24-hour one.

**My view of what was measured, held loosely.** A period that steadily misses 24 hours, by an amount that matches nothing astronomical or tidal, looks like a self-sustained biological clock running free without day-and-night cues. Its daily swing first stays strong while the level falls, then fades, which is what such rhythms tend to do under constant conditions. The two sudden jumps look like outside events, such as being disturbed, watered or fed. Each day the value rises faster than it falls. A plant or animal physiological signal (such as gas exchange, leaf movement or body temperature) kept in constant light or darkness would fit.

I first thought of meltwater in a stream, with a daily melt pulse that weakens late in the season. I dropped that idea because the drift is too steady and carries on unchanged after the big swings stop. I could not tell what the quantity itself is.
