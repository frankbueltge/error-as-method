I made "Wound Down", which lays the 61 days out as a wall calendar, one cell per day. It's a single still canvas with nothing loaded from outside the page. The page script passes a syntax check, but there was no browser here to render it. I checked the layout with a Python version of the same drawing code, so the title and labels drawn in the browser haven't been seen rendered.

**What I did first with the file.** I opened `record.jpg` and printed it as text, then measured every pixel column: how dark it is, and its highest and lowest ink. One thing set the limits of the whole project. There are about 4.9 hourly values per pixel column, so the image cannot show single hours. Each column blurs together about five hours.

**How I got to the form.**
- **A daily sawtooth.** The ink forms a regular pattern of steep rises followed by slow falls. The ink runs from x≈2 to x≈297, which gives about 4.85 px per day. At that scale the rise comes once every 24 hours.
- **Timing of the rise.** It lands in the late evening, roughly 21:00 to midnight, give or take one five-hour column.
- **Between rises.** The value falls fairly evenly for most of the day.
- **Longer runs.** Over weeks the daily highs and lows drift down in three runs: days 0–11, 11–27 and 27–43. Two big jumps reset them, around 26 October and 10–11 November.
- **The end.** From about 27 November the daily swings collapse to small ripples near the bottom, with a slight lift near the very end.

Because the rhythm that matters is the daily reset, I chose a Monday-to-Sunday calendar, starting from Wednesday 15 October. Each day's 1/61 slice of the image goes into its own cell, with each pixel column's ink stretched into a horizontal stripe. The result is one fall per cell, three runs across the weeks, and near-empty cells in the last three weeks. The whole record also runs unbroken along the foot of the page. I kept the source's own ink rather than drawing a smooth curve, so the five-hour limit stays visible instead of being covered by values I'd have to invent.

**Things you should know.**
- **No hourly values were recovered.** Nothing in the work claims hourly precision.
- **Dates and times are my assumptions.** I assumed the first value is at 00:00 on 15 October. Day boundaries come from where the ink starts and stops, and could be off by an hour or two.
- **Words and dates are added.** The source had none; I added the weekday names, dates, title and two captions.
- **The page is fixed at 1100 × 800.** It scales down on narrower screens.

**My view of what was measured, held loosely.** It looks like something with a reservoir, topped up once a night at a fixed time and drawn down steadily through the day. A battery, a tank, or stored heat such as a night-storage heater or a hot-water cylinder would all fit. It doesn't look like weather. Weather cycles are smooth and peak in the afternoon, not jump up at a set clock time, which points to a timer or schedule. The collapse to ripples from about 27 November reads like the daily top-up stopping, either switched off or failing. It also coincides with the lowest level in the record, which could mean whatever was being stored ran out.

A side note: 26 October 2025, the first big reset, was the day European clocks changed. The image is far too coarse to show whether that matters.
