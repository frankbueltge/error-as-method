I made a single still image: `/tmp/claude-0/s137/mk/m05/work/index.html`, with `/tmp/claude-0/s137/mk/m05/work/NOTE.md` beside it. The work is titled **Sixty-One Falls**. Its data is drawn straight into inline SVG paths, so the page loads no other file and needs no network. At 1100×800 it rendered as intended in a headless browser. My scripts, intermediate data and screenshots are in `/tmp/claude-0/s137/mk/m05/scratch`.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at it, then at enlarged crops, and dumped the dark pixels column by column. The ink runs from x=2 to x=297, so each pixel column holds about 5 hourly values. The obvious feature is a regular sawtooth with about 4.9 pixels per tooth, which is one tooth per day. Its size swells and shrinks over the record. There is a dip around days 9–11, a sudden surge around day 27 that dies away over about two weeks, and then about 18 days near the bottom where the daily swing nearly vanishes.

**How I got to the form.**
- **First attempt to recover hourly values.** I tried taking the dot centres in each column and guessing their order in time. This produced false mini-sawtooths inside columns. The picture's resolution simply doesn't hold the hourly order.
- **Series I actually used.** I switched to the ink-weighted mean height of each column, interpolated to 1,464 hours. This keeps the daily cycle, slightly smoothed (each column averages about 5 hours), and the overall level faithfully.
- **Period check.** Folding the series at different periods gave a strong peak at exactly 24 h, with nothing at 23.5 or 25 h, nor at the tidal periods of 12.42 and 24.84 h. Averaged over the record, the daily cycle is lowest at about hour 17 of the record's own count and highest at about hour 4.
- **The form.** I cut the record into 24-hour lengths starting at that daily low and stacked them as 62 ridgelines, with the first day at the top. There are 62 rather than 61 because the first and last lengths are partial; every one of the 1,464 values is drawn. Each day partly covers the one before, which shows through faintly. The daily pulse becomes the repeated shape of each line, and the larger story reads from top to bottom.

**What the commissioner should know.**
- **The values are a reconstruction.** They are read back from a 300×90 JPEG, so they are approximate, especially within a day.
- **No units or scale.** The vertical scale is relative to the picture's own height, not to real units.
- **The day boundary is mine.** The 24-hour cut is anchored to the data's own daily low, not to clock midnight, which I don't know.
- **The surge is diluted.** The day-27 surge appears as one more tall crest rather than a clear standout, because it's about as high as the early days' crests and the column averaging softens its peak.

**My view of what was measured (speculative).** I think it is most likely discharge or water level in a stream fed by snowmelt or glacier melt, from late summer into autumn. The evidence in the file:
- **Daily cycle.** It is strictly 24-hour, not tidal, which points to the sun.
- **Shape of each day.** The value rises fairly quickly and recedes more slowly, which fits a meltwater hydrograph.
- **Swing tracks level.** The daily swing grows and shrinks with the overall level, as melt runoff does on warm versus cold days.
- **The surge.** A sharp surge followed by a long, smooth fall looks like a rain flood followed by recession.
- **The ending.** The daily cycle dies out into a slowly sinking, almost flat baseline, as when melt stops.

Air or soil temperature is the main alternative: it also has a 24-hour cycle and could flatten under snow cover. But a temperature record wouldn't usually show that sharp spike decaying over two weeks. I avoided naming the quantity in the work itself; the title "Falls" only hints at water.
