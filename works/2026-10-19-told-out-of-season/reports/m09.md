I made a single still image called **Sixty-one Strokes**. Each of the 61 days in the record is drawn as one tapered ink stroke on warm paper, and the original hourly dots sit faintly behind them. The page draws itself on a canvas and needs no other files; a headless-browser check at 1100 × 800 showed it loads nothing but itself.

**What I did first.** I looked at the picture, enlarged it, and listed the dark pixels in every column. The pattern was clear before any analysis: a strong daily cycle. In each day there is a steep rise within a few hours, then a slow fall over the next 18–20. The gap between peaks is about 4.85 px, which is 24 hours at this scale.

**From the file to the form.**
- I worked out which hour each pixel column stands for (the dots run from x≈2 to x≈297 for 1,464 hours).
- I gave every dark pixel to a 24-hour cell. Each cell starts 3 hours before the recorder's midnight, so every daily rise sits at the start of its cell.
- For each day I took the high, the median and the low of its pixels.
- My first two versions re-placed all the pixels into day cells. They didn't work: a day's five pixel columns overlap, so the days blurred into one texture.
- So each day became one stroke instead: broad at the day's high and narrowing to a point at its low, like a pen pressed down and then lifted. That copies the sharp rise and slow fall.
- The real pixels stay visible as faint hourly marks behind the strokes.
- The only text is a small caption with the two end dates.

**Things you should know.**
- The values come from a small JPEG, so their accuracy is limited to roughly ±1 pixel, which is about 1/80 of the full range. No numbers or units were available, so the work shows shape and proportion only.
- Each source pixel column covers about 5 hours, so I couldn't recover individual hours. The faint marks are the plot's pixels, not 1,464 rebuilt values.
- The strokes leave out the shape of each day between its high and its low, and the start of the first day and end of the last are partial (because of the 3-hour shift).
- **What the record shows:**
  - a strong daily pulse from 15 January to mid-February
  - a two-day damped dip around 24–25 January
  - a few low days around 8–10 February, then the highest value in the record on about 11 February
  - a steady fade in both size and level until about 28 February
  - an almost flat stretch after that
  - small daily pulses coming back from about 10 March

**What I think was measured.** I'm fairly but not fully sure this is the water level or flow of a river fed by snow or glacier melt, in the southern hemisphere's late summer. This rests on four things in the file:
1. The cycle is exactly 24 hours and rises steeply then falls slowly. That is the classic daily shape of a meltwater stream: melt arrives quickly in the afternoon, then drains away overnight.
2. The pulses are large in January and die out by the end of February. That fits melt shutting down as autumn comes on, which only makes sense for January–March south of the equator.
3. The damped days look like cloudy or cold weather.
4. The single tall peak on about 11 February, followed by a lower level that drifts down, looks like rain on top of the melt.

The low days don't follow a weekly pattern, which argues against a human cause such as releases from a dam. A mixed tide is less likely: tides don't lock to exactly 24 hours, and they would show a two-week cycle that isn't there. If the recorder's clock is UTC, the rise comes around 22–23 h. That is plausible for a stream some distance below a glacier, but the timing is too rough to say where it is.
