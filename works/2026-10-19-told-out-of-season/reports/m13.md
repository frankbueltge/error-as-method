I've made the work, "Every Day, Until". It is a single canvas drawing at 1100 × 800 with no animation and no outside resources, and the data is embedded in the page. It's in `/tmp/claude-0/s137/mk/m13/work/index.html`, with the note in `/tmp/claude-0/s137/mk/m13/work/NOTE.md`. Working files (scripts and preview PNGs) are in `/tmp/claude-0/s137/mk/m13/scratch`. No browser was available, so I checked the page by running its script against a stand-in canvas and by drawing the same layout in Python. I have not seen the page itself rendered.

**First step with the file.** I looked at it, then read its pixels column by column. Each pixel column holds about 5 hours of data, so one day is about 4.85 columns. That made a daily cycle visible: a regular sawtooth with one tooth per day.

**From the file to the form.**
- **Reading the dots.** Following individual dots through the compressed image didn't work; the traces scattered. Instead, for each column I took the top and bottom dark pixel, ordered them so each column joins the next, and interpolated hourly values in between. This is honest to the image's real resolution (about 5 hours), but the shape within each day is a reconstruction, not recovered hourly data.
- **What the raw pixels show.** In each cycle the value rises fast, within one column (about 4–5 hours or less). It then falls more slowly, over about 12 hours, with short plateaus at the top and bottom.
- **First form tried.** I tried one line per day, stacked down the page. At that width each day became a flat hump, so I dropped it.
- **Final form.** One thin vertical stroke per hourly value, folded into nine week-long rows from Monday to Sunday. Height and colour (cold slate to ember) both show the value. Each day becomes one swell, and weekday patterns line up in columns.

**Things you should know.**
- **Weekdays are assumed.** The Monday–Sunday rows assume the first value is midnight on 15 January. The page says nothing about clock time, because the time of day can't be pinned down. Across the record the daily peak drifts about 5 hours earlier, which a one-pixel error in where the plot area starts and ends would also cause.
- **The value scale is relative.** 0 is the lowest point in the record and 1 the highest; the real units and range are unknown.
- **The colours suggest heat.** The cold-to-ember colouring leans towards my guess about what was measured (below). If you'd rather the work not hint at that, a neutral colour ramp is a one-line change.

**What I think was measured.** I think it is most likely an indoor air temperature in a room heated once a day, for example by a stove or fire lit at the same hour each day. That is a view, not a finding, and other things that are topped up daily and slowly run down would also fit (a hot-water tank, for instance). It rests on these features of the file:
- **Daily shape:** a quick rise to a short plateau, then a slower, levelling fall, which looks like heat being added and then lost.
- **Two gaps where people were away:** the daily cycle flattens on Thursday to Saturday, 23–25 January, and on Saturday to Sunday, 8–9 February.
- **A warm-up after the second gap:** the largest peak in the record comes right after it.
- **A season ending:** the daily peaks get smaller through February, and the cycle stops around 26–27 February. After that the values stay low with only small daily wiggles, as you'd expect if the heating had been switched off for the season.
