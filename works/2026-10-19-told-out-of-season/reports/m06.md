I made a work called **"Once Around"**: a single circle that lays the whole 61-day record clockwise in one turn, so the daily swings stand out as spikes that go quiet near the end. It opens as a still image at 1100 × 800 and loads nothing else. I rendered a screenshot and it looks as intended.

**What I did first.** I looked at the picture, then turned it into a grid of characters by darkness so I could read it column by column. I measured the top, bottom and darkness-weighted middle of the dots in each column. Then I split the 300 columns into 61 day-slices of about 4.9 pixels each and measured each day's swing and average height.

**What the file shows:**
- A strong swing every day, of about 35–48 of the 90 pixels, from 15 October to roughly 25 November.
- About 24–25 October, two days where the swing shrinks to about 17 pixels and the values drop to the lowest of that period.
- About 11 November, a single surge to the highest values in the record, followed by a slow downward drift for about two weeks.
- From about 27 November, the daily swing collapses abruptly to 3–14 pixels. The values stay low and nearly flat until 14 December.

**How I got to the form.** Time runs around the circle and larger values sit further out. Each day takes a sixtieth of the circle, so the daily swing becomes one tooth: six weeks of teeth, a gap at the late-October dip, a tall spike in mid-November, then a smooth worn edge for the last 18 days. A ring of short strokes inside shows each day's swing, with those last 18 in red. The only words are the two end dates at the seam at the top and a three-line caption.

**What you should know:**
- Only about 4.9 hourly values fall into each pixel column of the picture, and within a column their order in time cannot be recovered. So I did not rebuild a guessed series of 1,464 values. The work draws the picture's own inked pixels, each scattered at random (with a fixed seed, so it never changes) inside its own cell of time and value. It shows exactly the detail the file holds and no more.
- The day boundaries assume the plot runs edge to edge across the 300 pixels, so the positions of individual days could be slightly off.
- JPEG speckle below a darkness threshold is dropped.

**My view of what was measured.** My guess, which I can't confirm, is temperature near the ground or of the air at a mid- or high-latitude site in the northern hemisphere. The record rests on four things:
- a clear 24-hour cycle;
- a general downward drift from mid-October into December;
- short cold-looking spells and one warm surge, typical of weather passing through;
- most tellingly, the sudden end of the daily cycle around 27 November, with values settling low and flat.

That last change looks like snow arriving and insulating the sensor, or the start of persistent overcast or very short winter days. A solar-charged battery voltage would also fit, but less well. I couldn't check any of this, since I used nothing outside the folder.
