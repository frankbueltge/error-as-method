I made a still work called **"Run-out Groove"**. It is in `/tmp/claude-0/s137/mk/m11/work/index.html`, with the note in `/tmp/claude-0/s137/mk/m11/work/NOTE.md`. Scripts and previews are in `/tmp/claude-0/s137/mk/m11/scratch/`.

**What I did first.** I looked at the image, then turned it into a text grid of dark and light pixels and measured the ink in each pixel column. The dots run from x≈3 to x≈296, which is about 0.2 px per hour, so each 1-pixel column holds about 5 values. A periodogram of the column means peaks at 4.82 px. That matches 24 hours (4.81 px), so the cycle is exactly daily. A tidal period (about 4.97 px) is ruled out.

**From the file to the form.**
- **Recovering the values:** for each column I split the ink into as many height quantiles as there are hours in it. I put them in rising or falling order depending on the columns either side. This gives an approximate hourly series, which is embedded in the page (about 10 KB, no external resources).
- **The disc:** a dark disc on paper carries one spiral groove, one turn per day, running from the rim inward. Each hour sets how wide and how bright the groove is there.
- **What shows up:**
  - The daily cycle lines up into a paler side and a darker side of the disc.
  - The three high-running periods read as bands, like tracks on a record.
  - The one surge is a bright single ring.
  - The quiet last 18 days spiral into a dim centre, like an LP's run-out groove. The file being called "record" suggested the idea.
- **Label and checks:** there is a small label in the centre: the title, "61 turns · 1 464 hours" and "one turn per day". No browser was available, so I checked the composition with a Python re-render of the same geometry and smoke-tested the page script in Node.

**Things you should know.**
- **Coarse timing within a day:** about 5 values share one pixel column, so the recovered series only places events to within about ±2–3 hours. Their order within a column is my guess. The day-to-day structure is reliable; fine detail within a day is not.
- **Where the turns start:** the zero angle (top) is simply the first hour of the record. I don't know the clock time it corresponds to.
- **Value scale:** values are pixel heights. There are no units or absolute scale.
- **Not rendered in a real browser:** the only check of the final page was the Python re-render, so give it one look at 1100 × 800.

**What I think was measured (tentative).**
- **The pattern in the file:**
  - There is a strong daily cycle. Each day the value stays low for about 5 hours, climbs quickly, stays high for about 10 hours, then falls slowly.
  - It comes in three periods: days 1–8, about days 12–26 and days 28–43. Each starts high and then declines, and between them are low, flat days (about days 9–11 and 25–26).
  - On day 28 there is one sharp surge, the highest value in the record.
  - From about day 44 the daily cycle almost disappears and values sit near the bottom. The small daily peak that remains comes about 6 hours later than before.
- **My best guess:** discharge or water level of a stream fed by snowmelt.
  - Melt drives the steep rise and slow fall each day.
  - Warm spells and cold breaks make the periods.
  - The surge could be rain on snow.
  - When the snow runs out, the stream drops to low baseflow. A weak, shifted daily signal like that is typical when it is driven by plants drawing water rather than by melt.
- **Other possibilities:** a sun- or occupancy-driven quantity, such as a room or greenhouse temperature, or CO₂ that rises during the day. It is a hypothesis from the shape alone. The work does not depend on it.
