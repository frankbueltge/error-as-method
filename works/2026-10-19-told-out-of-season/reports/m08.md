**What I did first with the file:** I looked at it, then enlarged it and dumped a pixel grid. The ink covers columns 2 to 297 and rows 5 to 84, so each pixel column is about 5 hours and each day about 4.85 pixels. The single hourly values can't be recovered at this resolution. What can be read is how much ink each day leaves at each height.

**How I got to the form:** I mapped columns to hours and folded the column centres by time of day. That confirmed a strong 24-hour cycle with a fixed shape: a quick rise lasting about one column (around 5 hours), then a slow fall over 15 hours or more. The cycle stays large until about 25 November, collapses to almost nothing over the next few days, and comes back faintly around 7–14 December. There is one sharp spike, the highest value in the record, around 10–11 November. Under all of this the level falls overall.

For the work, I summed the ink of each day's columns row by row and smoothed it lightly. I gave every day the same area, because every day holds 24 hours. Each day became a symmetric figure whose width at each height shows how long the value stayed there. So the swinging days turn into tall needles and the quiet late days into dark seeds. A faint line joins the daily medians. The only text is a title line, the two end dates and week ticks.

**Things you should know:**
- The figures are built from JPEG ink, not from the true values. Each width is approximate, and where dots overlap in the late flat stretch the ink saturates. Making all days equal in area corrects for most of that, but not all.
- The time of day in the image may be off by about ±5 hours, because one pixel column is about 5 hours.
- Day boundaries assume the first dot is 15 October 00:00 on the record's clock. Because the daily rise falls late in the day, part of the 10 November spike shows up as a hairline on the figure for the day before.

**My view of what was measured (tentative):** I think it is most likely a near-surface temperature driven by the sun (air, ground or a shallow surface) at a place moving into winter. Four things support this:
- the clean 24-hour period;
- the uneven cycle shape, with a fast rise and a slow fall, which is typical of daytime heating and night cooling;
- the falling level from October into December;
- the daily cycle nearly ending around 26 November. That fits something cutting off the sun's effect on the sensor, such as snow cover, lasting cloud or fog, or the sun dropping too low.

One fact argues against a simple local reading. On the record's clock the daily peak falls around 00–04 h and the low around 16–18 h. For a sun-driven temperature, that means the timestamps are not local time, for example UTC at a site roughly 9–12 hours from Greenwich. Otherwise it is a quantity that peaks at night. A runoff record is the other candidate I considered: its fast-rise, slow-recession shape matches, and a stream fed by daily melt would peak late and fall to flat baseflow in winter. I would not commit to either without knowing the units or the place.
