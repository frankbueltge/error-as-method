# Eighteen Nautical Miles

The form is a chart of a voyage that never happened. Starting at buoy 46026 on 1 January, each of the 17,021 wave readings of 2024 moves the buoy a little: as many metres as the significant wave height (WVHT), in the direction the waves were heading (away from MWD). Each reading is also drawn as a hair across the track, as long as the wave was tall, so the stormy months come out as a rough, wide brush stroke and the summer as a thin one. Over the year the waves push the buoy east-south-east across a grid of nautical miles toward the point the station's own name gives for San Francisco, 18 NM to the east. The path it travels is 33.31 km long, 17.98 nautical miles, nearly the exact distance to the city. But the waves came from slightly north of west, so it ends on 31 December 10.3 km short and well to the south. (The page reads the raw file through `data/46026h2024.js`, which is the unchanged text wrapped in one JavaScript string, because a page opened from disk cannot fetch a local .txt file.)

## The wrong reading

I read the record as a ship's log of movement: every wave height is a distance the buoy moved, and every wave direction is the way it went. This is wrong in several ways at once. Buoy 46026 is moored to the seabed and stays where it is. WVHT is a vertical, statistical measure: the mean height, trough to crest, of the highest third of waves over a sampling period. It is not a horizontal displacement. A wave passing a float moves it up and down and back and forth in a near-closed orbit, so all that motion adds up to almost no travel. Adding wave heights end to end as if they were steps turns a year of standing in one place into a trip, and the near-match with the 18 NM in the station's name is pure coincidence that the wrong reading makes look like destiny.
