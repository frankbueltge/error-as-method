# Carried by the Swell

The work sets buoy 46026 loose from its mooring off San Francisco and lets the waves of 2024 carry it. Each of the 17,021 wave reports in the record (mean wave direction and dominant period) becomes a leg of a voyage: the buoy moves the way the waves are travelling, at the speed of their crests (g·T/2π), until the next report, stepping across the curved Earth. The North Pacific swell, arriving almost always from the west-north-west, pushes it east-south-east, and on a sphere a steady east-south-east heading spirals into the South Pole. So the buoy crosses the hemisphere within weeks and spends the rest of the year, about 545,000 km of travel, circling the Pole more than a thousand times. It is lifted back out toward the latitudes only when the southern-hemisphere swell of the northern summer turns the waves around. The image is a south-polar view with distance from the Pole on a logarithmic scale, so that the knot at the centre can be seen at all. The year's track is drawn in fine lines coloured by month, and the one small ring near the edge marks where the buoy actually was the whole time.

## The wrong reading

I read the wave columns as a current: as if the waves were water that flows and takes floating things with it at the speed of the crests. That is wrong. A surface gravity wave carries energy, not water. A floating object under a passing swell mostly moves up and down in a small orbit and ends up nearly where it started. Stokes drift moves it forward slightly, at centimetres per second, not at the roughly 18 m/s phase speed of a 12-second swell. The reading also ignores that 46026 is moored and cannot travel at all, and it treats each wave report as holding steady until the next one. The voyage it produces is impossible: thirteen times around the Equator, then a thousand turns around the South Pole for a buoy that never left the Gulf of the Farallones.
