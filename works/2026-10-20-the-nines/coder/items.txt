I1
I read the wave columns as the buoy's own log of travel, as though it rode each swell it measured. That is wrong in three ways. First, 46026 is moored: it is anchored to the seabed and goes nowhere, and the record describes the water passing it. Second, a wave is not a current. The energy and the crest shape move at the phase speed, but the water, and anything floating in it, mostly goes round in small circles. A floating object drifts only slowly with the Stokes drift, centimetres per second and not tens of metres. Third, MWD is the direction the waves come *from*, and DPD is a property of the spectrum's peak, not the speed of anything. Turning these into a heading and a speed treats a description of the sea as the buoy's own movement.

I2
I read every record as a single wave: one wave of height WVHT and period DPD, then the next record as the next wave. That is wrong in three ways. First, WVHT is the *significant* wave height of a 20-minute sample. It is a statistic, the mean of the highest third of all the waves in that sample, so no single wave has to be that tall. Second, DPD is the period where the spectrum holds the most energy, not how long any one wave lasted. Each record therefore stands for a hundred or more real waves, not one. Third, the records are about half an hour apart, and laying them end to end throws away all the time between them. The real year of 8,784 hours shrinks into the 53.6 hours it would take for one imagined wave from each record to pass the buoy.

I3
I read the file's fill values as data. NDBC writes 99, 999 or 9999 (with its decimals) in a column when there is no measurement. In this file the air temperature, dew point, visibility and tide columns are never measured. Wind and pressure are missing most of the time, and wave values for about two thirds of the rows. So I took the placeholders at face value, as if the air 18 miles off San Francisco had been 999 °C all year and the pressure ten times what it is on Earth. That is wrong because these numbers stand for "nothing was recorded here", not for anything that was recorded. Taking them literally turns the record's silences into its loudest and most constant signal. The values that really were measured end up as small noise underneath.

I4
I read the wave columns as a current: as if the waves were water that flows and takes floating things with it at the speed of the crests. That is wrong. A surface gravity wave carries energy, not water. A floating object under a passing swell mostly moves up and down in a small orbit and ends up nearly where it started. Stokes drift moves it forward slightly, at centimetres per second, not at the roughly 18 m/s phase speed of a 12-second swell. The reading also ignores that 46026 is moored and cannot travel at all, and it treats each wave report as holding steady until the next one. The voyage it produces is impossible: thirteen times around the Equator, then a thousand turns around the South Pole for a buoy that never left the Gulf of the Farallones.

I5
I read the record as a ship's log of movement: every wave height is a distance the buoy moved, and every wave direction is the way it went. This is wrong in several ways at once. Buoy 46026 is moored to the seabed and stays where it is. WVHT is a vertical, statistical measure: the mean height, trough to crest, of the highest third of waves over a sampling period. It is not a horizontal displacement. A wave passing a float moves it up and down and back and forth in a near-closed orbit, so all that motion adds up to almost no travel. Adding wave heights end to end as if they were steps turns a year of standing in one place into a trip, and the near-match with the 18 NM in the station's name is pure coincidence that the wrong reading makes look like destiny.

I6
I read the file's missing-value codes as measurements. In the NDBC format, `999` in the wind-direction column and `99.0` in the speed and gust columns mean "no data". The anemometer on 46026 stopped reporting during the 4 February storm and stayed silent for the rest of the year (the barometer went with it, which is why pressure reads `9999.0`). Taken at its word, the file says that storm never ended: it rose to 99 m/s, about 356 km/h and well beyond any recorded hurricane, and blew for eleven months from a bearing of 999 degrees, which is just under three full turns, or 279 degrees, roughly west. That is wrong because those numbers describe the instrument's silence, not the air. Wave height, water temperature and the buoy's later records all show an ordinary year of Pacific weather going on underneath.
