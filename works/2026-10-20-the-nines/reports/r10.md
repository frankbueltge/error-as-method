I made "Passage of a Moored Buoy": a single still image in which the San Francisco buoy rides every swell it recorded in 2024.

**The wrong reading.** I treated the buoy's 17,021 wave records as its own travel log. For each record, the buoy moves the way the swell was going (the mean wave direction plus 180°). Its speed is the deep-water speed of a wave with the reported dominant period, and it keeps going until the next record arrives. The legs are stepped across a spherical Earth.

**What the picture shows.** The whole globe is drawn as one disc seen from the South Pole, with the North Pole as the rim. The track starts at the real mooring and adds up to about 544,000 km, roughly 1.42 times the distance to the Moon. The swell mostly comes from the west and northwest, so the buoy spirals down into the South Pole and circles there. Only swell from the south, mostly in summer, pulls it out in wide loops. The line is coloured by month and gets thicker with wave height. A red ring marks where it actually stayed all year.

**Why it is wrong.** The buoy is anchored to the seabed and goes nowhere. A wave carries energy, not water: a floating object only drifts centimetres per second, not tens of metres. And the wave direction column says where the waves come from, not a heading.

**The data file.** The wind columns only cover January and early February, so the wave columns were the ones that run all year. The page cannot read the .txt file directly when opened from disk, so I added a second copy of it, unchanged but wrapped as a JavaScript string: `data/46026h2024.js`. `tools/mkjs.js` makes that copy from the original, which is untouched.

The viewer reports no page errors. The final render is `renders/04.png`.
