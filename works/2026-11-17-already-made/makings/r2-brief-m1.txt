Your proposal 1: The Instruments Were Silent
  1. Lo que sucede aquí, no se queda aquí (What Happens Here Doesn't Stay Here) — José Carlos Martinat, 2017. An algorithmic system continuously scrapes and processes Venezuelan news media on the political crisis, printing the results onto an ever-thickening 'informational floor' of paper — a literal accumulation of data that overtakes any single reader's capacity to comprehend it.
  2. Biblioteca de la No-Historia (Library of Non-History) — Voluspa Jarpa, 2011. A thousand hand-bound books reproduce declassified CIA files (via the U.S. National Security Archive / FOIA) on Operation Condor and Southern Cone dictatorships — the seals, stamps, and redaction blackouts of the originals kept intact, so the archive's own censorship becomes the artwork's visible content.
  3. Here the River Lies — Debbie Ding, 2010–2015. Crowdsources 1,500+ oral micro-histories of a vanished river onto a hand-drawn map and deliberately refuses to distinguish verified memory from invented story, indexing both into the same archive as a challenge to the evidentiary claims of official heritage data.

Your proposal 2: Undo
  1. Not allowed for algorithmic audiences — Kyriaki Goni, 2023. A big number of audiences online nowadays is mainly algorithms. Algorithms are trained on the auditory information that is produced and uploaded by humans. In Not allowed for algorithmic audiences, a digital assistant situated in an Athenian apartment exhibits an odd behavior.
  2. Mythmachine — Sahej Rahal, 2022. An AI-driven virtual biome of post-human creatures listens to and choreographs itself from ambient audio feedback captured live in the gallery, proposing folklore-derived 'other' intelligences as an alternative to extractive machine learning.
  3. Brute Force — Vinzenz Aubry, Robert Schnüll, 2026. A precise demonstration of the dead-internet thesis: software multiplies faster than anyone can read it, while we keep performing the gestures that once meant authorship.

Your proposal 3: Uphill Since 1958
  1. Climate temperature-anomaly sonification — Harlan Brothers, 2025 (ongoing project, latest coverage Nov 2025). Converts global land-based temperature anomalies since 1880 (Berkeley Earth dataset) into one musical note per year against a 1950-1980 baseline, making 150 years of warming audible as an accelerating, increasingly dissonant climb rather than a chart.
