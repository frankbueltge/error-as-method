I made a still image called **"Sixty-One Turns"**: a 24-hour clock face with 61 rings, one per day. 15 July is the outer ring and 13 September is the centre. Each hour is filled with one of 18 stepped colours, running from pale straw (high) to deep blue (low), on a dark ground, with a small key and a few lines of text beside it. I checked it in headless Chromium at 1100×800. The page is self-contained: the 1,464 hourly levels are embedded as a 1,464-character string and drawn as SVG by inline JavaScript.

**What I did first with the file:** I looked at it, then printed its pixels as text. That showed something the thumbnail hides: the dots sit on only 18 evenly spaced horizontal levels, about 4.6 px apart. The quantity moves in fixed steps, and from hour to hour it mostly flickers between two neighbouring steps.

**From the file to the form:**
- For each of the 300 pixel columns I measured how much ink sat on each of the 18 levels, giving a 300 × 18 table.
- I averaged across the levels in each column, weighted by ink, and folded the result by hour of day. This gave a clean daily cycle: lowest near 08:00 and highest near 19:00, about ±1.2 steps around the daily mean. Under it is a slide of roughly ten steps over the two months, with a few sudden drops of 2–3 steps within a day.
- To get hourly values I interpolated between columns and then snapped each hour to a level. The snapping carries the leftover fraction on to the next hour (error diffusion), so the flicker between neighbouring steps is kept in the right proportions.
- I chose the clock face because a ring per day lines the hours up. The daily cycle then shows as a cool wedge on the morning side and a warm one on the evening side, and the seasonal slide reads as the rim darkening inward.

**Things you should know:**
- **The hourly values are reconstructed, not read off the picture.** A 300 px image of 1,464 points gives about 4.9 hours per pixel column, and the JPEG blurs the dots, so the exact hour-by-hour sequence can't be recovered. The levels, the daily phase and the overall shape are solid; the value at any single hour is an informed estimate. The smoothing also probably makes the daily swing look somewhat smaller than it really is.
- **The clock times assume the record starts at 00:00 on 15 July.** I don't know the time zone or the actual start hour, so "08:00" and "19:00" are times on the record's own clock.
- **There are no units.** The key says only "higher", "lower" and "18 steps". Two very rare outliers (a single high reading near the start, and the lowest steps late on) are blended into their neighbours.

**My view of what was measured:** probably a temperature of something with thermal inertia, such as lake or river water, or soil a little below the surface, read by a sensor that rounds to coarse steps (perhaps 0.5 °C or 1 °F). The view rests on four things in the file:
- There is a strong 24-hour cycle that is smooth and small compared with the seasonal change. Air temperature would swing much more within a day.
- The peak comes late, around 19:00, and the low around 08:00. That is a lag of several hours behind the sun, which is typical of water or soil warming and cooling slowly.
- The steady decline from mid-July to mid-September matches late-summer cooling.
- The occasional abrupt drops look like cold spells or heavy rain.

It could instead be something else that is driven by the daily and seasonal cycle, such as a water level pulled down by evaporation. Nothing in the file proves which.
