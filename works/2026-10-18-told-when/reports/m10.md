The work is done: **"Eighteen Rings"**, a single still image in `/tmp/claude-0/s135/mk/m10/work/index.html`, with its note in `/tmp/claude-0/s135/mk/m10/work/NOTE.md`. I checked it in headless Chromium at 1100 × 800: it loads no outside files or network resources and throws no errors.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at it, then printed every pixel as text. That showed the main thing about the record: the values are not continuous. Every dot sits on one of 18 evenly spaced heights (about 4.59 px apart), so the quantity only ever took 18 values. Dark pixels also come in clusters about five columns apart, one cluster per day, which is a strong daily cycle.

**How I got from the picture to the form.**
- **Turning pixels back into values.** Each pixel column covers about five hours, and each dot is about 2 px wide.
  - I measured how wide one day is on the picture by finding the strongest repeat in the pattern: 4.82 px per day. That puts the plotted data between about x = 2.3 and x = 296.2.
  - Measured that way, the high and low points fall at the same hours of every block of 24 hours across the whole record. My first guess at the span had made them drift by about 10 hours.
  - I rebuilt the 1,464 hourly values with a path-finding step and then a search that makes the redrawn dots cover every dark spot in the picture. The redrawn version matches the original closely.
- **The form.** One turn of a spiral is one day, read clockwise from the top. Each of the 18 values is a ring.
  - The line follows a ring for each hour and steps sideways to the next ring on the hour.
  - Inside each ring, July lies on the outer edge and September on the inner. The ink shifts from brown to blue-black over the two months.
  - The result has two visible features:
    - Every turn bulges toward the same hours of the day, which is the daily cycle.
    - The line moves inward ring by ring in a few sudden drops.
  - The data is stored in the page as a 1,464-character string.

**What you should know.**
- **The order of hours is reconstructed.** Which rings were used, and roughly when in each day, comes straight from the picture. The exact order of hours inside each five-hour pixel is my estimate. The page says this in small type, and so does the note.
- **The hour labels are not real clock times.** The 0/6/12/18 marks count hours within each 24-hour block, starting at the first value. I don't know what clock or time zone the recording used.
- **One value hits the top ring.** It appears once, around the second day. It could be a real reading or a stray mark in the compressed image.

**My view of what was measured (a guess, not a finding).** I think it is most likely an air or near-surface temperature, recorded to whole units, at a cool and probably coastal Northern Hemisphere site. It rests on these things in the file:
- **The 18 equal steps.** They look like a reading rounded to whole numbers, as hourly weather reports round temperature to whole degrees.
- **One asymmetric peak per day.** The value stays low for several hours, rises sharply over about 3 hours, then falls slowly. That is how sun-driven heating behaves. The low comes around hours 5–9 of each block and the high around 14–19. If those were local times the rise would come late, which suggests the times are not local, perhaps UTC.
- **The season.** Daily averages fall by about 9 steps from mid-July to mid-September.
- **The drops.** The decline happens in sudden steps of 2–4 values, around 1–3 August, about 20 August, about 27 August and about 8 September, with a rise again in late August. Those look like weather fronts.
- **The size of the swings.** The daily swing is only 3–5 steps, small next to the seasonal fall. If one step is 1 °C, that points to a mild, sea-influenced climate.

Nothing in the file proves it is temperature. A water temperature with the same rounding would fit nearly as well.
