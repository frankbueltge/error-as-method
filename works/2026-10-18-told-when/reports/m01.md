The finished piece is called "Eighteen Steps Down". It's a single still image made of 61 columns, one per day. Each column has a short horizontal bar (a tread) on every one of 18 evenly spaced levels the reading touched that day, darker the longer it stayed there. Faint hairlines mark the 18 levels, small counting ticks run along the bottom with a longer one every 7 days, and there's a short title and caption. I couldn't open it in a browser here, so I checked the layout with a Python copy of the drawing code and checked the script's syntax with Node.

**What I did first.** I opened the image and printed it as text characters, pixel by pixel. That showed something the plot doesn't announce: the dots never fall between levels. They sit on exactly 18 evenly spaced rows about 4.6 px apart, so the instrument could only report 18 distinct values.

**How I got from the file to the form.**
- I measured how much ink each of the 296 used pixel columns has on each of the 18 levels.
- I looked for repeating cycles in that data. There is a strong one with a period of 296 px ÷ 61, which is one day. There is no weekly cycle.
- I cut the columns into 61 equal day-slices and recorded, for each day, how much of it the reading spent on each level. Those values (61 × 18, coarsened to the digits 0–9) are embedded in the page.
- My first attempt kept detail within each day. It came out broken and noisy, so I switched to one solid tread per day per level.

**What the person who commissioned this should know.**
- **The hour-by-hour values can't be recovered.** Each pixel column holds about 5 hours of readings, and dots on the same level overlap. What the image reliably gives is which levels were visited within a span of a few hours. The tread shading is an approximate estimate of time spent, not a count of hours.
- **The day boundaries are arbitrary.** They start at the first reading, whose time of day is unknown, so they aren't calendar midnights.
- **The 7-day ticks are only for counting.** They don't stand for anything in the data.

**My view of what was measured.** I think it's most likely a temperature recorded at a coarse fixed step (for example whole degrees) during a northern-hemisphere autumn, probably in a sheltered or buffered spot rather than open air. Water, soil or an unheated room would fit. This rests on four things in the file:
- **A strong daily cycle and no weekly one.** That points to the sun rather than human routine.
- **A swing of only about 2–3 steps per day**, which is small.
- **A steady fall of about 10 of the 18 levels over the 61 days.**
- **Sudden drops lasting several days, followed by partial recovery.** These look like weather spells, around days 18–22, 36–44 and 52–55.

The fall is the part that suggests autumn. The rest is inference from shape alone: the image has no units or scale.
