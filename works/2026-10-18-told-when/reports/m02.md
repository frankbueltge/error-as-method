I made "Sixty-One Rows", a still image of a knitted piece built from the record. It has one stitch per hour, one 24-stitch row per day and 18 yarn colours, and it loads no external files.

**What I did first:** I looked at the image and then printed its pixels as an ASCII map, without guessing at the data. Two things stood out straight away:
- **Discrete levels:** every dot sits on one of exactly 18 horizontal levels, about 4.59 px apart. The instrument could only report 18 distinct values in this window.
- **A daily rhythm:** the dot clusters repeat about every 4.8 px, which is one day across the image's width. At the high levels the dots fall at one point in that repeat, at the low levels at another, so each day has a rise and a fall.

**How I got to the form:**
1. **Recovering the values:** I worked out where the level lines sit, the size and darkness of a single dot (about 2×2 px, partly transparent, so overlapping dots look darker) and where each hour falls left to right. I then fitted 1,464 hourly level values to the picture by drawing candidate series and comparing them with the image.
2. **Fixing the time scale:** my first fit made the daily peak seem to drift seven hours later over the record. Measuring the daily period straight from the image (4.82 px) showed my horizontal spacing was slightly off. After I corrected it, the daily pattern stays put for the whole record and the fit error fell to 60 (a blank image scores 795).
3. **Choosing the form:** quantized levels, an hourly rhythm and a slow descent suggested a "temperature blanket", the craft tradition of knitting one row per day in colours keyed to a reading. The 18 levels become 18 yarns. The piece is turned on its side so time runs left to right as in the original, with loose fringe at the start and end. Under it is a row of 18 yarn balls, each sized by its stitch count.

**What you should know:**
- **Individual hours are approximate.** The source packs about 5 hours into each pixel column, so single hourly values are a best fit, not a readout. The level of each day, how much it swings, the time of day of the high and low, and the overall trend are robust.
- **Start of day is assumed.** I assumed the record begins at the start of a day. The file can't confirm this, but it fits well: the low comes 2–5 hours in and the high 11–16 hours in.
- **No units or scale.** The page shows only relative levels. Nothing in the file gives the values, the units or the size of one level step.
- **Not published.** The page was checked headless at 1100×800 with no errors and no network requests, but it exists only as a file in your folder.

**My view of what was measured:** probably hourly outdoor air temperature over two autumn months (or spring, if southern hemisphere), recorded at whole-unit resolution, likely 1 °C. This is inference, and it rests on:
- **The shape of each day:** values are lowest in the hours before what would be dawn, rise in the morning, peak in early to mid afternoon and decline slowly through the evening.
- **The size of the swing:** the daily swing is about 3–5 levels, small enough to suggest a cloudy or maritime climate.
- **The trend:** daily averages fall by about 10 levels over 61 days. The fall comes in steps: a plateau of about 17 days, a sharp drop around day 18, a partial recovery around days 28–35, then a steep fall from about day 36 to day 44. It looks like weather systems passing on top of a seasonal decline.
- **Flat days:** some later days barely move (for example days 41–42, only 2–3 levels), which looks like overcast weather.
- **The steps themselves:** 18 discrete values fit a sensor or display that rounds to whole degrees.
