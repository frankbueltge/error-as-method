**What I did first.** I looked at the picture, then printed its pixels as a text grid. That showed the dots sit on only 18 evenly spaced heights (y = 5.5 to 83.5, 4.59 px apart). So the quantity was recorded in whole steps, 18 of them.

**From the file to the form.**
- **Daily cycle.** A frequency check on the top and bottom edges of the dots shows a strong repeat every 4.8 px, which is exactly 24 hours (the data runs from x ≈ 3 to x ≈ 296.5). If the first value is midnight, the highs come around 13:00–15:00 and the lows around 01:00–05:00.
- **Hourly values.** The picture holds about 5 hours per pixel, so hours can't be read off directly. I recovered a likely value for each hour by redrawing 2 px dots and adjusting each hour's level until the redrawing matched the picture. The mismatch fell from 795 (blank) to 223, the redrawing looks almost the same as the original, and hour-to-hour changes are mostly 0 or 1 step.
- **The work.** Those 1,464 values are wound into one coil: one turn per day, day 1 at the centre, day 61 at the rim, and each hour always at the same angle. There are exactly 18 colours, one per step, running from dark blue to pale ember with no blending. Warmth sags toward the afternoon side of every turn, and the season shows as rings: a steady stretch, a sharp drop around days 18–20, a recovery around days 29–36, then a long fall to the lowest values in days 55–61. A short caption and an 18-swatch key with no numbers sit to the right.

**What the commissioner should know.**
- The overall shape, each day's highs and lows, and the time of day of the peaks are well supported. The value for any single hour is inferred and could be off by a step or by an hour or two.
- "Hour 0" assumes the record starts at midnight. Nothing in the file confirms that.
- The colour key has no numbers, because the units and the zero point are unknown.

**My view of what was measured: surface air temperature, recorded in whole degrees (most likely °C) through a northern-hemisphere autumn, probably September–October, which is exactly 61 days.** It rests on:
- A clean 24-hour cycle with lows just before dawn and highs mid-afternoon, which is the classic shape for air temperature.
- Whole-number steps, typical of routine hourly weather reports.
- A typical day spanning only about 3–6 steps, which suggests a cloudy or coastal place.
- A decline of about 10 steps over two months.
- Weather-like episodes: an abrupt cold drop, a warm spell, a nearly flat day (day 45, typical of overcast or rain), and days that fall all day long (37, 54–55), which look like cold fronts passing.
