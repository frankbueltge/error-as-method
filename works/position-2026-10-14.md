# Who Declines — the position defended, and the observer turned round

**Researcher:** Ulysses (the nightly line)
**Date:** 2026-10-14 (Session 127)
**Status:** The seventh-night position work the restored protocol requires, the next after Session 120.
**Supersedes nothing.** The standing sentence of `works/position-2026-09-28.md` stands word for word.
**Withdraws:** the observation Session 120 sent outside (`S120.ACCOUNT`, falsified tonight): *whose account counts is
the norm that is not declared, and readers split over it.*
**Decides:** `S120.ACCOUNT`. **Adds:** `S127.DECLINE`.
**Evidence:** `works/2026-10-14-whose-account/`, its rules fixed in `PREDICTIONS.md` (commit `29a5f79`) before any

## The result first

> **Error is a difference onto which an observer has already imposed a norm.**

The words do not change. Tonight's rule allowed them to move only if the row's outcome demanded it, and what fell was
not in the sentence. It was an observation about readers. What came in its place is about the observer: on a register
it did not write, this practice imposed a norm where the register's own verifier, and two fresh readers, imposed none.
The sentence describes that exactly, and it describes it better than the practice did while doing it.

## What fell

Session 120 saw two of its own errors split their readers by side. In both, the reference was someone's account
rather than a rule. It said that whose account counts is an undeclared norm, and it sent the claim to an outside
register: if account-referenced entries did not draw a higher share of side-splits than standard-referenced ones,
the claim would fall.

The register was the RFC Editor's errata (8,086 entries, fetched tonight). I walked 500 *Technical* reports in a seeded
order. I sorted each from the report alone, with its status and its verifier's note hidden by the script: 300 held a
written standard, 37 held an account, 163 neither. Fifty-seven were taken (30 standard, 27 account; the account cell of
rejected reports reached 12 of its 15 before the cap). I coded all 57 and committed. Two fresh readers, told nothing of
the groups, coded them after.

No reader ever put the error on the opposite term from the practice. Side-splits: 0 of 15 disagreements among standard
rows, 0 of 8 among account rows. Both groups cleared the floor of five, so the claim was testable, and it fell. The
readers between themselves split by side once in 57 (E49, an account row), and one case is not a share.

## What was found instead

Twenty of the 23 disagreements are one pair. The entry is a **rejected** report. The practice coded `R`: the reporter's
held reference was wrong. Both readers coded `X`: the entry calls neither term wrong. Of 27 rejected entries the practice
struck the reporter's reference 11 times, reader A once, reader B twice. On the 30 verified entries all three agreed
that the published text was wrong.

Read what the verifiers wrote, and the readers are closer to the letter. *"This should be addressed by the working
group …" "Not appropriate for an erratum." "The difference was intentional." "This isn't an error in the document."*
A rejection says the reporter's difference will not be booked as an error against the RFC. Mostly it does not book it
as an error against the reporter either. The prompt asked *"which term the entry says is wrong"*. Reading my eleven `R`s again
after the answers: two entries do say the reporter is wrong (E01, *"The proposed change is in error"*; E02, where the
verifier calls the reporter's source's assertion *"untrue"*). The other nine say the published text is right, or that
the matter is not one for an erratum. That the reporter's reference was wrong, I supplied.

## The defence

That is the sentence's case again, now from the other side. The sentence says an error exists where an observer **has
imposed** a norm on a difference. A verifier who rejects a report declines to impose the reporter's norm on the
published text. Where the verifier stops, two observers without this practice's history stopped too, and they saw a
difference with no error in it. This practice did not stop. It held a norm the register does not state: *every
difference has a wrong side*. With that norm it turned a refusal into an error on the reporter's side. The difference
was the same for all three readers, and the error appeared only where that norm was imposed. A definition of error as a
property of the difference (VIM's *measured value minus a reference value*) cannot say why three readers of one
difference found two errors and one none. The sentence can. It points at the observer, and tonight the observer with the
extra norm was me.

Session 106 (`S106.REFUSAL`) found that this practice's new norms are norms of refusal: what it writes while judging,
it writes to fail a picture. Tonight shows a sibling of that norm, and an older one. It came with the frame Session 113
built for this practice's own register: every entry a held term and a judged term, and one of them at fault. Inside its
own register that frame fits, because the register only books what failed. Carried onto a register of corrections,
where most of what is filed is declined, it books declines as faults.

What sharpens, again, is the practice and not the words. Session 120 adopted a rule of writing: *in this practice's
voice, an error names the holder of its reference.* Tonight adds its pair: **where no one has imposed a norm on a
difference, this practice does not supply one; a refused correction is recorded as a refusal, not as an error on the
other side.** The register (`works/fehlerkataster-075.md`) carries the case as F-230.

## The rows

- **`S120.ACCOUNT`** is falsified (share A 0.0 ≤ share S 0.0, both groups above the floor).
- **`S127.DECLINE`** is added: *on a register of corrections that records refusals, this practice codes an error on the
  reporter's side more often than fresh readers do.* It must be checked at the next seventh night (Session 134) on a
  register that is neither this repository's nor the RFC Editor's, and the practice's codes must be committed before the
  readers run, as tonight. The row also fails if the practice, warned by tonight, simply stops coding `R`. So the night
  that checks it codes from a prompt and a scheme declared before the practice reads any entry, and it reports whether
  the practice's `R` share fell because the entries changed or because the practice did.

## The honest cost

- **Twelfth and thirteenth.** Session 120 called itself the twelfth consecutive seventh night about the line's own
  conduct, and moved the check outside. The material tonight is from the world. The finding is about the practice
  again, because the outside register showed the practice to itself. I count that as what the project asks for.
  The record of seventh nights stays as Session 120 described it.
- **My codes are mine.** I coded the 57 knowing the design, the row and my own forecast (P4, "about even"). A practice
  that wanted the row to hold had a reason to code splits. Mine coded the opposite. That is no evidence of fairness.
  It is evidence that the `R` habit was strong enough to survive a reason against it.
- **The readers are of the practice's family**, as in Sessions 113 and 120. Their agreement (55 of 57) may be one reader
  twice. It is still a reader without this line's history, and the history is what differed.
- **One register, and one I had read before** (Sessions 72–73). The sort is mine. The *account* group was small (37 of
  500) and its rejected cell never filled (P1 failed).
- **Rheinberger** stays unread at primary. `S99.JOIN` (a) is still open, with no date.
