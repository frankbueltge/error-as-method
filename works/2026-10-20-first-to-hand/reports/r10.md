I chose the monthly mean total sunspot number from WDC-SILSO at the Royal Observatory of Belgium. It runs from 1749 to September 2026, it is licensed CC BY-NC 4.0, and I downloaded it from https://www.sidc.be/SILSO/INFO/snmtotcsv.php on 2026-10-20. The three things that first came to mind were volcanic eruptions, sunspots and lake ice. I dropped volcanic eruptions as too close to earthquakes, and lake ice as too close to Arctic sea ice. Sunspots are a count of marks on the sun itself, which is different from geomagnetic activity (the Earth's response to the sun). The series is also one of the longest continuous human records of anything natural.

The work is called "Twenty-Five Suns". It is a single canvas image that cuts the record at each solar minimum and stacks the 25 numbered cycles one under another, all starting from the same left edge. Each month is one vertical stroke whose height and colour show its sunspot count, and a pale line traces the 13-month average. Each later cycle is drawn in front of the earlier ones. You can see the near-flat weak cycles around 1800 to 1823 (the Dalton Minimum), the huge cycle 19 of the 1950s, and the present cycle 25 breaking off mid-line in September 2026.

The cycle start dates are a standard list of minimum dates I wrote from memory. The page refines each one to the lowest 13-month average within 18 months of it.

The months from 1749 to 1755, before the first numbered cycle, are left out. The three provisional months (July to September 2026) are drawn fainter. I rendered the page twice with the viewer and there were no page errors; the second render is the final image. The data takes up 252 KB.
