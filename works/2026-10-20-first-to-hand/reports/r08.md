I chose the eruption log of Old Faithful geyser in Yellowstone. It comes from GeyserTimes, a volunteer logging project, as the per-geyser archive file at https://geysertimes.org/archive/geysers/Old_Faithful_eruptions.tsv.gz, fetched 2026-10-20. The site says the data is under an "Open Source Database License".

My first three ideas were sunspots, Old Faithful and lake ice. Sunspots seemed too close to the excluded geomagnetic activity, and lake ice too close to Arctic sea ice, so I took the geyser. Its domain is like none on the list, and the record runs back to 1970.

The file has about 205,000 logged eruptions. I removed duplicate entries and kept only the waits between two consecutive eruptions that were both exactly timed: 99,548 of them. Each one also carries the length of the eruption before it, where that was recorded. That derived file is `data/intervals.js`, 1.5 MB. I deleted the raw archive.

The work, "The Wait at Old Faithful" (`index.html`), is a single still image on a canvas, with no libraries and no network. Every wait is a faint grain of light: across by date from 1970 to 2026, and up by how long it lasted. They build up like steam into one column per summer of observers. The colour shows the eruption before the wait: blue after a short one, amber after a long one, pale grey where the length wasn't recorded.

- **The two-part rhythm:** in the 1970s and 1990s, short eruptions are followed by short waits and long ones by long waits.
- **The rise:** the whole cloud moves up from about 65 and 80 minutes to 90 minutes and more. That happens across the gap of years with no records around the 1983 Borah Peak earthquake, which I marked.
- **The gaps:** the empty stretches (about 1980–91 and 1999–2006) are labelled as having no exactly timed pairs, not filled in.
