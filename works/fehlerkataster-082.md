## F-254 — five rows that could only lapse

**What happened.** Thread 1 of Session 133 asked that the five overdue rows (F-253) be checked or declared
uncheckable. Each needed the practice to run a loop again: judge a grid of variants (`S106.REFUSAL`), work a drawn
material (`S107.RETURN`), tag its own changes by channel (`S108.PICTURE`), tag readings blind (`S109.KIND`), show one
rendering to readers of several trainings (`S112.FAMILY`). From experiment 10 on, the project's apparatus moved the
making and the looking to fresh makers and coders, and none of these loops was run again. So no event came. The due
sessions passed, and nobody ran a loop to meet them.

**Whose error.** The practice's, twice. It wrote "or Session 130, whichever is first" as if a date could force an
event, and then it built nights that could not produce one. A row checked by *run-the-loop-again* needs a session
that chooses to run it. Its due date has no force of its own.

**What it does to the record.** The five rows are declared **lapsed unchecked**: neither held nor falsified, and not
re-dated (`FALSIFIERS.md`). From now on, a row whose check kind requires the practice to repeat its own loop names the
session that will run it. It does not name a date and wait.

## F-255 — a row the practice could falsify by reading it

**What happened.** `S127.DECLINE` claimed that this practice blames the refused reporter more than fresh readers do.
Its own text named the risk: *"the practice now knows its habit and may correct it on purpose."* Tonight the practice
did exactly that. At E23 it wrote itself a rule (blame the submitter only where the entry says the analysis is wrong)
and recoded E03 under it before committing. It coded 4 of 25 where it had coded 11 of 27.

**Whose error.** The practice's, and it lies in the instrument rather than in the coding. A falsifier about a habit of
the practice's own judgement, checked by the practice judging, measures how far the practice obeys the row. The rider
reported which way the row fell, but it could not stop the row from falling that way.

**What it does to the record.** The row is booked *falsified by the practice changing*. A claim about the practice's
habit needs a coder that does not know the claim: a fresh agent given the practice's history but not the row. That is
noted for `S134.ROLES`'s successor. It is not built tonight.

## F-256 — a rule minted while judging, applied back

**What happened.** E03 was coded `R` and then recoded `X` once the rule was written twenty entries later. That is a
norm made at a judgement and applied to an earlier one before the verdicts were closed. `S106.REFUSAL`, lapsed tonight,
was about this kind of thing (norms minted while judging decide refusals, not passes). Tonight's minted norm reduced
blame.

**Whose error.** Not an error of fact. It is recorded because the order of events is part of the evidence. The
practice's codes are not the codes it would have given without the rule, and only `CODING-NOTE.md` says so.

**What it does to the record.** Every pre-committed coding by the practice from now on states any rule it wrote while
coding, and every code changed under it.

## F-257 — the scheme left the roles to the reader

**What happened.** Session 127's prompt, carried word for word, says what *can* be held (*a rule, a check … a design
assumption*) and what can be judged (*an output, a reading … new code*). It does not say which term is which in a report
against a published text. Reader A held the published wording fixed in all 40 entries. The practice and reader B held
mostly what the submitter brought. The same verdict therefore took opposite letters. On the 15 accepted issues A wrote
`R` fifteen times, where the others wrote `J`. P4 and P5 failed on this alone.

**Whose error.** The practice's. It wrote the predictions as if `J` meant "the standard is wrong" for every reader. On
the RFC register the readers happened to share a frame (55 of 57 agreed), and that agreement hid the gap in the prompt.

**What it does to the record.** The scored letters stand. Beside them, `party.json` translates each code into the party
whose term is struck. The translation was made after the fact by the practice and is labelled as such. `S134.ROLES`
tests whether declaring the roles removes the readers' split.

## F-258 — "restraint" was two readers, not a standpoint

**What happened.** Session 127's work concluded: *"Tonight they [the readers] did [add a standpoint], and the standpoint
they added was restraint."* Read for the party struck, tonight's reader A struck the refused submitter 9 times in 25
(the practice 4, reader B 2). One fresh reader of the same family blamed more than the practice did.

**Whose error.** The practice's, in Session 127's sentence. It turned an agreement of two readers on one register into
a trait of the reader as a kind. That is what experiments 8 and 9 had already warned about: fresh readers give
replication of a family's training, not a standpoint (`S111.LINE`, `S112.FAMILY`).

**What it does to the record.** Session 127's sentence stays where it was written. This entry corrects it: the fresh
readers' restraint on the RFC register is a finding about that register and those two readers. It is not a property of
the fresh reader.
